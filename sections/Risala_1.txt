بسم الله الرحمن الرحيم 
بدان‌كه علوم حكمى چهار نوع است: اول رياضيات، دوم منطقيات، سوم طبيعيات، چهارم الهيات. و رياضيات چهار نوع است: اول ارثماطيقى و آنچه به خاصيت عدد تعلق دارد، و دوم هندسيات، و سوم هيأت افلاك، و چهارم علم موسيقى. و بزرگترين كسى‌كه در علم اعداد و خاصيت آن سخن گفته است فيثاغورس حكيم بوده است. و او چنان ياد كرده است كه نخست سخن در عدد آن است كه گوئيم اعم اسماء «شئ» است و به پارسى شئ «چيز» باشد. و «چيز» يا يكى بود يا بسيار. و يكى بر دو وجه بود: وجهى را «حقيقى» گويند و وجهى را «مجازى». و يكى به حقيقت آن بود كه وى را جزو نبود، و مهندس آن را «نقطه» خواند. و يكى به مجاز چنان بود كه گويند ده يكى، و صد يكى، و هزار يكى - چنانك در رقوم هند بيايد. و از دو تا صد تا هزار زبر يكهاست، بر سر يكديگر گرفته.

 و هرچه نام بسيارى بر وى افتد يا «عادّ» بود يا «معدود». و معدود بى‌عادّ نباشد هرگز، و عادّ بى‌معدود باشد. و عدد دو نوع بود: يكى را «صحاح» گويند و يكى را «كسور». و اصل همه عددها از يكى است كه بيش از دو است، و مبدأ همه عددهاست. و صحاح و كسور همه از وى خيزند و به وى بازگردند. و دو، دو يك باشند بر سر يك دگر گرفته، و همچنين صد هزار. و اما «كسور»، به لفظ‍‌ او را كسور گويند. و الا از روى حقيقت خود كسور نيست. زيرا كه آنچه «نصف» گويند آن دو باشد، از آنك چون در نيمه‌نگرى در آن حال كه جدا باشد آن يكى باشد بى‌خلاف، و همچنين ثلث و ربع و خمس الى مايتناهى. و اين معنى [را] محاسبان در حساب «نسبت» گويند. و اين جمله كه ياد كرديم چهار مرتبه باشد: آحاد، عشرات، ميات ، الوف. و آحاد از يكى باشد تا نه، و عشرات از ده باشد تا نود، و ميات از صد باشد تا نهصد، و الوف از هزار تا نه هزار. و الوف به‌مرتبۀ آحاد باشد تا چندان‌كه شود. و از آن است كه محققان گويند مرتبه سه است و الوف به مرتبۀ آحاد است. اما آحاد نه يك باشد بر سر يكديگر نشانده، و هريك را از اين نهگانه «عقدى» خوانند. و عشرات نه بار ده باشد تا نود، و آن نه «عقد» باشد، چنانك سى را سه عقد خوانند، و پنجاه را پنج عقد، و نود را نه عقد. و همچنين صد را يك عقد خوانند، و دو هزار را دو عقد تا چندان‌كه رسد.

 و بدان‌كه اين مراتب نه چيزى است كه شايد گفت كه از وجه ضرورت چنين است. بل كه وضعى است كه حكيمان نهاده‌اند موافقت آن را كه بيشتر موجودات چهارند. مانند طبع چهارگانه كه حرارت و برودت و رطوبت و يبوست است. و اركان كه آتش و هوا و آب و زمين است. و فصل چهارگانه كه بهار و تابستان و خزان و زمستان است. و اخلاط‍‌ چون خون و بلغم ] و صفرا و سودا. و بادها چون شمال و جنوب و صبا و دبور. و وتد چهارگانه چون طالع و سابع و وسط‍‌ السماء و وتد الارض. و جهت چهارگانه چون شرق و غرب و شمال و جنوب. پس آن موافقت را مراتب چهارگانه نهاده‌اند. و سبب آن‌كه بيشتر امور طبيعى چهار افتاده است آنچه مابعد الطبيعة است هم به چهار قسم است. چنانك ابتدا از واجب كنند. گويند: بارى‌تعالى، و عقل فعال، و نفس كلى فلكى، و هيولى اولى. و نسبت بارى‌عزّ و علا با موجودات جمله چنان است كه نسبت يكى با جمله عدد، و نسبت عقل چون دو، و نسبت نفس چون سه، و نسبت هيولى چون چهار. و اما از خاصيت اعداد يكى آن است كه اگرچه عددها بسيار باشند ايشان را از چهار مركب شايد كرد، يعنى از يكى تا چهار، بدين صورت: 4321. چنانك مثلا يكى بر چهار افزايى پنج شود، و اگر دو برافزايى شش شود، و اگر سه برافزايى هفت شود، و چون چهار بر چهار افزايى هشت شود، و چون دو و سه بر چهار افزايى نه شود، و چون يكى و دو و سه بر افزايى ده شود، و همچنين جملۀ عددها از يكى تا چهار مركب شايد كرد. و اگر كسى خواهد كه بداند كه بارى‌تعالى چگونه ابداع كرد عقل و نفس و هيولى را، بايد كه اين اصل كه گفتيم اعتبار كند. چه بارى - عزّ و علا - اول

 چيزى كه بيافريد از نور و وحدانيّت خويش جوهرى بسيط‍‌ بود كه او را «عقل فعّال» خوانند، همچنان كه ما دو را مركب كنيم به تكرير و نفس كل چون سه، همچنانكه بارى‌تعالى «عقل» را از نور پاك خويش بيافريد، از نور عقل جوهرى بيافريد و آن «نفس كل» است، و از نفس كل «هيولى» را بيافريد چون چهار. پس از هيولى به توسط‍‌ عقل و نفس همۀ خلايق را بيافريد. همچنانكه گفتيم كه از چهار همه عددها را تركيب شايد كرد اين مثال نگه دارد در حق واجب الوجود و عقل و نفس و هيولى. پس هرچه شريفتر است به منزلت «عقل» است، و هرچه شرف وى كمتر است از عقل به منزلت «نفس» است، و آنچه در زير وى است در عالم از موجودات به منزلت «هيولى». پس بايد كه بداند كه همچنانكه اعداد ما لا يتناهى از يكى پديد آيد و بازگشت همه با يكى بود، و يكى همان چنان است كه بود، نه از وى بكاست، و نه متغير شد، و نه از حال خويش بگشت، نه كه از وى پديد آمد، و نه كه به وى بازگشت، همچنين بارى - عزّ و علا - اصل همه موجودات است و بازگشت همه بدوست و متغير نشود و در وى زيادت و نقصان نيايد. و همچنانكه يكى در همه عددى هست و به همه شمارى محيط‍‌ است بارى تعالى به همه موجودات محيط‍‌ است و اول همه است و آخر همه است. و همچنانكه يكى در يكى بى‌مانندست بارى‌تعالى را مانند نيست. و چنانكه هيچ عددى به يكى نماند و وى به هيچ عددى نماند بارى - عزّ و علا - به هيچ موجودات نماند و هيچ ] موجودى به وى نماند. پس گوئيم كه كسور را اصل هم از يكى است، از آنك نصف يكى باشد از دو، ثلث يكى باشد از سه، و ربع يكى باشد از چهار، و خمس يكى باشد از پنج، و همچنين تا مالايتناهى. و هر عددى را از كسور و صحاح خاصيتى هست، و خاصيت آن باشد كه هيچ چيز ديگر در آن شركت ندارد با وى از اجناس.

 اما خاصيت يكى آن باشد كه اصل همه عددهاست و همه عددى از وى آيد و همه حسابها را بشمارد از زوج و فرد. و خاصيت دو آن باشد كه اول عددى است مطلق و نخست زوجى است. و خاصيت سه آن است كه اول فردى است و ثلث از وى خيزد. و خاصيت چهار آن است كه اول عددى است مربع كه از ضرب دو اندر دو آمده است، و اول عددى «مجذور» است. و خاصيت پنج آن است كه چندان‌كه وى را در چيزى ضرب كنى خويشتن را نگاه دارد، چنان‌كه پنج در پنج بيست و پنج بازآيد، و بيست و پنج در بيست و پنج ششصد و بيست و پنج بود، و همچنين مالايتناهى، و اول عددى «داير» ست. و خاصيت شش آن است كه همچنين خويشتن را نگاه دارد و اول عددى «تام» است. و خاصيت هفت آن است كه اول عددى «كامل» است. و خاصيت هشت آن است كه اول عددى «مكعب» است. و خاصيت نه آن است كه اول عددى «فرد» است كه او را «جذر» است. و خاصيت ده آن است كه اول عددى است از عشرات. و خاصيت يازده آن است كه اول عددى «اصمّ‌» است. و خاصيت دوازده آن است كه اول عددى «زايد» است. و از خاصيت همه عددى آن است كه نيمۀ دو كنار خويش باشد، مثال اين چنانكه پنج كه كناره‌هاى او شش است، و چهار، جمله ده بود و نيمۀ وى پنج باشد. و چهار كه دو كنارۀ او سه و پنج بود كه هشت باشد و چهار نيمۀ وى بود.و يكى را دو حاشيه نيست. بلى او را يك كناره هست و آن دو است و يكى نيمۀ وى باشد. و اما «عدد تام» آن باشد كه چون اجزاى وى جمع كنى بى‌كسرى همچند خويشتن بود. چون شش كه او را نصف و ثلث و سدس است. چون هر سه [را] جمع كنند شش باشد، مثلا سدس شش يكى و ثلث دو و نصف سه جمله

### Summary of the First Epistle on Arithmetic  
In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  

Know that philosophical sciences are of four kinds: first, the mathematical; second, the logical; third, the natural; and fourth, the divine. The mathematical sciences are also of four kinds: first, arithmetic, which pertains to the properties of numbers; second, geometry; third, the configuration of the celestial spheres; and fourth, the science of music. The greatest scholar to have spoken on numbers and their properties was the sage Pythagoras. He expounded that the first discussion concerning number is that we may call it by the most general name, "thing" (shay’), which in Persian is "chīz" (thing). A "thing" is either one or many.  

The "one" is of two aspects: one is called "real" (ḥaqīqī), and the other is called "metaphorical" (majāzī). The "real one" is that which has no parts, and the geometer calls it a "point." The "metaphorical one" is when we say "ten is one," "a hundred is one," or "a thousand is one"—as in the Indian numerals, where digits are placed above one another from two to a hundred to a thousand.  

-33-  

Whatever is named "many" is either a "counter" (‘ād) or a "counted" (ma‘dūd). The "counted" never exists without a "counter," but the "counter" may exist without the "counted." Number is of two kinds: one is called "whole" (ṣiḥāḥ), and the other is called "fractional" (kasūr). The origin of all numbers is the "one," which is beyond duality and is the principle of all numbers. Both wholes and fractions arise from it and return to it. Two is two ones placed above one another, and so is a hundred thousand.  

As for "fractions," they are called so linguistically, but in reality, they are not fractions. For what is called "half" is two, because when you consider half in its separated state, it is one without dispute. The same applies to a third, a quarter, a fifth, and so on indefinitely. Mathematicians call this concept "ratio" (nisba).  

The categories we have mentioned are of four ranks: units (āḥād), tens (‘asharāt), hundreds (mi’āt), and thousands (ālūf). The units range from one to nine, the tens from ten to ninety, the hundreds from one hundred to nine hundred, and the thousands from one thousand to nine thousand. The thousands follow the rank of units indefinitely. Hence, scholars say there are three ranks, with thousands following the units.  

The units are nine ones placed above one another, and each of these nine is called a "knot" (‘uqdā). The tens are nine instances of ten up to ninety, and these nine are called "knots," just as thirty is called three knots, fifty five knots, and ninety nine knots. Similarly, one hundred is called one knot, and two thousand is called two knots, and so on.  

-34-  

Know that these ranks are nine in number, and one might say this is due to necessity. However, it is a convention established by the sages in accordance with the fact that most existing things are fourfold. Examples include the four temperaments—heat, cold, moisture, and dryness; the four elements—fire, air, water, and earth; the four seasons—spring, summer, autumn, and winter; the four humors—blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile; the four winds—north, south, east, and west; the four cardinal points—ascendant, descendant, midheaven, and earth’s foundation; and the four directions—east, west, north, and south. Thus, they established four ranks to align with this harmony.  

The reason most natural matters are fourfold is that metaphysical realities are also divided into four. For instance, they begin with the Necessary Being (al-wājib), saying: the Almighty (al-Bārī), the Active Intellect (al-‘aql al-fa‘‘āl), the Universal Celestial Soul (al-nafs al-kullī al-falakī), and Prime Matter (al-hayūlā al-ūlā). The relation of the Almighty to all existents is like the relation of the "one" to all numbers. The relation of the Intellect is like "two," the Soul like "three," and Matter like "four."  

Among the properties of numbers is that although numbers are infinite, they can be composed from four—that is, from one to four—as follows: 1, 2, 3, 4. For example, adding one to four yields five; adding two yields six; adding three yields seven; adding four to four yields eight; adding two and three to four yields nine; and adding one, two, and three to four yields ten. Likewise, all numbers can be composed from one to four.  

If one wishes to understand how the Almighty originated the Intellect, the Soul, and Matter, they must consider this principle. For the Almighty first created from His light and oneness a simple substance called the "Active Intellect," just as we compose "two" by repetition. Then, just as the Almighty created the "Intellect" from His pure light, He created from the light of the Intellect another substance, the "Universal Soul" (three), and from the Soul, He created "Matter" (four). Then, through Matter, mediated by the Intellect and the Soul, He created all beings—just as we said all numbers can be composed from four.  

-35-  

This example holds true for the Necessary Being, the Intellect, the Soul, and Matter. Thus, whatever is nobler is in the rank of the "Intellect," whatever is less noble than the Intellect is in the rank of the "Soul," and whatever is beneath it in the world of existents is in the rank of "Matter."  

Therefore, one must know that just as infinite numbers arise from the "one" and all return to it—while the "one" remains as it was, neither diminished, changed, nor altered, neither arising from itself nor returning to itself—so too is the Almighty the origin of all existents, to whom all return, unchanging, without increase or decrease.  

Just as the "one" is present in all numbers and encompasses all counting, the Almighty encompasses all existents. He is the First and the Last. Just as the "one" is incomparable, the Almighty has no equal. Just as no number resembles the "one" nor the "one" any number, the Almighty resembles no existent, nor does any existent resemble Him.  

Thus, we say the origin of fractions is also the "one," for half is one of two, a third is one of three, a quarter is one of four, a fifth is one of five, and so on indefinitely. Every number, whether fractional or whole, has a unique property, and this property is such that no other thing shares it in kind.  

-36-  

The property of "one" is that it is the origin of all numbers, from which all numbers arise, and it counts all calculations, whether even or odd. The property of "two" is that it is the first absolute number and the first even. The property of "three" is that it is the first odd, and a third arises from it. The property of "four" is that it is the first square number, resulting from multiplying two by two, and the first "quadratic" number.  

The property of "five" is that no matter how much you multiply it by something, it retains itself. For example, five times five is twenty-five, twenty-five times twenty-five is six hundred twenty-five, and so on indefinitely. It is the first "circular" number.  

The property of "six" is that it similarly retains itself and is the first "perfect" number. The property of "seven" is that it is the first "complete" number. The property of "eight" is that it is the first "cubic" number. The property of "nine" is that it is the first "odd" number that has a "root." The property of "ten" is that it is the first of the tens. The property of "eleven" is that it is the first "prime" number. The property of "twelve" is that it is the first "abundant" number.  

Another property of all numbers is that half the sum of its adjacent numbers equals itself. For example, five’s adjacent numbers are six and four, totaling ten, half of which is five. Four’s adjacent numbers are three and five, totaling eight, half of which is four. The "one" has no two adjacent numbers—rather, it has one adjacent number, which is two, and half of that is one.  

-37-  

A "perfect number" is one whose parts, when summed, equal itself without fraction. For example, six—whose parts are half (three), a third (two), and a sixth (one)—sums to six. This is the first "perfect number."  

As for "seven," the "complete number," its significance is that it encompasses all numerical meanings. Every number is either even or odd, or a combination of both. In seven, all meanings exist: it is both the first even (two) and the second even (four), as well as the first odd (three) and the second odd (five). If you combine the first odd and the second even, you get seven. If you combine the first even and the second odd, you also get seven.  

"Eight," the "cubic number," is so because two times two is four, and four times two is eight. It is the first "solid" number, for no number is solid unless it is cubic. A solid is that which has length, width, and depth, and eight is the first number with these dimensions. Every line has two ends, representing two parts. When multiplied in width, it becomes four; in length, eight. This illustrates the progression from point to line, surface, and solid.  

We also say that every number is either "even" or "odd." An "even" is that which can be divided into two equal halves, while an "odd" cannot be so divided without fractions. Subtracting or adding one to an even makes it odd, and adding or subtracting one from an odd makes it even.  

"Even generation" begins with two and doubles it—such as two, four, six, eight—or multiplies it further, like two, four, eight, sixteen. "Odd generation" begins with one and adds two successively—such as one, three, five, seven, nine.  

-38-  

"Even" is of three kinds: first, "oddly even" (zawj al-fard); second, "evenly even" (zawj al-azwāj); and third, "evenly odd" (zawj al-zawj wa-l-fard). "Absolute even" applies to all pairs.  

"Oddly even" is when halving it results in an odd—like six, ten, thirty, etc. "Evenly even" is when it can be halved repeatedly until reaching one—like four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two, sixty-four, etc. "Evenly odd" is when it can be halved more than once but not down to one—like twenty-eight and similar numbers.  

"Odd" is either "prime" (fard awwal) or "composite" (fard murakkab). A "prime odd" is divisible only by one—like three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen. A "composite odd" results from multiplying one odd by another—like three times three is nine (divisible by one and three), or three times five is fifteen (divisible by three and five), and so on indefinitely.  

Alternatively, we may say numbers are of three kinds: "perfect" (tāmm), "abundant" (zā’id), and "deficient" (nāqiṣ). A "perfect number" is one whose parts sum to itself—like six, twenty-eight, four hundred ninety-six, and seven thousand one hundred twenty-eight. An "abundant number" is one whose parts sum to more than itself—like twelve, twenty, sixty, etc. For example, twelve’s parts are half (six), third (four), quarter (three), sixth (two), and twelfth (one), totaling sixteen.  

A "deficient number" is one whose parts sum to less than itself—like four, eight, ten, etc. For example, eight’s parts are half (four), quarter (two), and eighth (one), totaling seven—one less than eight. Similarly, ten’s parts are half (five), fifth (two), and tenth (one), totaling eight—two less than ten.  

-39-  

Another natural property of numbers is that if you sum from one to any desired number, the total equals adding one to the original number and multiplying by half of it. For example, summing one to ten: add one to ten (eleven), multiply by five (half of ten), yielding fifty-five. This applies to all even numbers.  

For odd numbers, halve the number (e.g., five becomes two and a half), round up (three), then multiply by the original number (fifteen). For summing one to eleven: halve eleven (five and a half), round to six, multiply by eleven (sixty-six).  

Multiplication is the doubling of one number by another—like six times seven is forty-two, whether taken as six sevens or seven sixes.  

Multiplication is of three kinds: whole by whole, fraction by fraction, or whole and fraction combined. More precisely, multiplication is of six types:  
1. Whole by whole,  
2. Whole by fraction,  
3. Whole and fraction by fraction,  
4. Fraction by whole and fraction,  
5. Whole and fraction by whole and fraction,  
6. Fraction by fraction.  

There is much to say about multiplication and its implications, but this is not the place. We have given this example as an introduction to arithmetic.  

Multiplication is of ten kinds:  
1. Units by units,  
2. Units by tens,  
3. Units by hundreds,  
4. Units by thousands,  
5. Tens by tens,  
6. Tens by hundreds,  
7. Tens by thousands,  
8. Hundreds by hundreds,  
9. Hundreds by thousands,  
10. Thousands by thousands.  

-40-  

Beyond these, there are no other types except derivatives.  

When multiplying "knots" of tens, hundreds, or thousands, we reduce them to units—like twenty as two, thirty as three, fifty as five. For hundreds, each knot is taken as one—three hundred as three, seven hundred as seven, nine hundred as nine. Similarly, thousands are reduced—two thousand as two, six thousand as six, nine thousand as nine. Thus, in this table, we take each knot accordingly.  

When numbers are multiplied by themselves, they form a "square" (murabba‘), not a "quadratic" (majdhūr). For example, three times three is nine, a square but not a quadratic.  

-41-  

Four times four is sixteen, and so on. When multiplying different numbers—like five by four—it is a "rectangle" (mustaṭīl), not a quadratic, for a quadratic has equal sides.  

-42-  

For example, six by nine or seven by three are rectangles. Any square number, whether quadratic or not, when multiplied by another number, yields a "solid" (mujassam)—having length, width, and depth. For example, five times four:  

-43-  

Then multiplied by four, imagine standing in the air—this number has width, length, and depth, totaling eighty.  

If a "quadratic" number is multiplied by its root, the result is a "cube" (muka‘‘ab). For example, four is a square and quadratic, its root is two; multiplying four by two yields eight, a "cube." Thus, two is the root of four, four is its square, eight is its cube, and two is its cube root.  

Multiplying sixteen by its root (four) yields sixty-four, the cube of four. The square of four is sixteen, and four is the root of sixteen. In summary, length, width, and depth constitute a solid, but a "cube" is when all three dimensions are equal—having six equal surfaces, twelve parallel edges, eight solid angles, and twenty-four plane angles.  

If a square is multiplied by a number less than its root, it is called "tabular" (labanī)—having length and width greater than depth, six surfaces, twelve edges, eight solid angles, and twenty-four plane angles.  

"Columnar" (bayrī) applies when depth exceeds length and width—like two times two (four, a square) multiplied by five or more (e.g., ten or one hundred).  

-44-  

If not a square—like two times three (six)—multiplied by a larger number, it is also "columnar," with six surfaces: two opposite squares (equal-sided) and four rectangles, with depth exceeding length and width, like a well.  

This concludes our introduction to arithmetic.  

Know that the purpose of this epistle is for the wise to recognize that counting and similar matters are all "accidents" (a‘rāḍ). A single person may have multiple accidents simultaneously, and it is certain that accidents do not subsist by themselves—they require a "substance" (jawhar) to inhere in. All these inhere in the soul.  

If the human soul were an accident, these accidents could not inhere in it, for accidents cannot inhere in accidents. Thus, it is proven that the human soul is a substance, not an accident, and thousands of accidents inhere in it. Further, we establish that it is a simple substance.  

Thus, it is proven that the human soul is a substance, and we have Quranic evidence for its existence as a substance:

- "As for he who feared the standing before his Lord and restrained the soul from desires, Paradise will be his refuge." (79:40–41)  
- "O contented soul, return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing. Enter among My servants, and enter My Paradise." (89:27–30)
