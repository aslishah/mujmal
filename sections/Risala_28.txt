### Translation of the Summary of the Thirteenth Epistle on Natural Sciences Concerning Human Capacity for Knowledge  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

In this epistle, our purpose is to gather the sciences and demonstrate the extent of human capacity for knowledge—what can be grasped according to one’s understanding and which sciences lie beyond human comprehension. First, we affirm what has been established in the preceding epistles: that man is composed of body and soul, the body belonging to the lower world and the soul to the higher world. We have previously stated that the earth and all upon it are insignificant compared to the heavens and the cosmic order, and some may grow weary in reading this epistle. Yet truth must be spoken, especially in matters of proof.  

Thus, we say: When the wise observer reflects upon his own body, the five senses, and the other faculties within the heart, liver, and brain, he realizes that the eye, as one of the senses, cannot perceive all things. When the sun rises at dawn, the eye cannot bear its gaze. Likewise, in intense heat, the eye cannot look directly at the sun due to its overpowering radiance, nor can it discern the minute particles of starlight, the flame of a lamp, or a tiny mosquito, nor can it fully grasp objects of immense size. Similarly, hearing cannot detect extremely loud sounds, nor the faint footsteps of an ant—one due to excessive volume, the other to excessive softness. Only what lies between these extremes can be perceived.  

The same applies to taste: when one quality dominates—such as the heat of honey or the coldness of vinegar—the taste cannot distinguish between them due to that dominance. But when the heat dissipates and returns to moderation, both the sweetness of honey and the sourness of vinegar become discernible. As for smell, when confronted with an overpowering fragrance or stench, it fails to differentiate, and the organ itself is harmed.  

Likewise, the human intellect can grasp only intermediate levels of knowledge. If a scholar were to spend his entire life seeking to comprehend the abstract realities in the heavens or the celestial spheres, he would fail. The same applies to understanding the darkness on the moon’s surface, or knowing which peoples existed ten thousand years ago, what they did, or what will transpire fifty thousand years from now—such things cannot be known through rational demonstration. However, what lies between these periods—such as minor conjunctions occurring every twenty or thirty years (minor, intermediate, major, and greater)—can be known, though reasoning about them remains difficult.  

Similarly, the Necessary Being, in His majesty, is beyond the reach of intellect; He cannot be encompassed. Likewise, the "abstract intellect" (the form separate from matter) cannot be fully grasped as it truly is, even if one constructs rational proofs for it. What remains beyond our comprehension is like the creature in the ocean: just as we know nothing of their perfection, they know nothing of ours, though they may have witnessed many wonders. Yet what remains unseen is far greater. The same holds for spirits and the nature of the celestial dominion (*malakūt*)—we cannot perceive them in their true perfection.  

Thus, our path is to give thanks to God, the Exalted, for what we *can* perceive through the senses and the intellect, and for what lies beyond our grasp, we must follow the guidance of the sent prophets, so that our worldly affairs may be ordered and our hereafter secured. This suffices.  

Now, when we say that the intellect is incapable of nothing within its domain—that it reigns over all that is subordinate to it—this is certainly true. But what lies above it, such as the essence of the Divine, cannot be fully known, not due to any deficiency in the intellect, but because of God’s overwhelming majesty. What the intellect and soul *can* apprehend—and what man, as established in philosophical texts, is master of—cannot be fully realized only because of the body’s veiling and obstructive nature, not due to any inherent weakness or opacity in the intellect itself.  

What we know of intelligible realities is not even one-hundredth of what the intellect *could* know of what exists and what will be in the universe. If there were doubt in this, then the superiority of spirits over bodies would be negated. What we *do* grasp of intelligible truths comes only after great effort, ascending from sensory perception through disciplined training, like climbing a ladder. Evidence for this lies in the soul of the prophet and the soul of the soothsayer: when questioned, they answer without their bodies or senses knowing what they say. Likewise, when the soothsayer or the madman speak, their senses are absent—their intellect is partly engaged in governing the body and partly attuned to the celestial realm, aware of all universal truths. It imparts only what the listener can bear, translating it into speech.  

If the senses did not obstruct intelligible truths, the abstract intellect, the celestial dominion (*malakūt*), the Necessary Being, and all we have mentioned would be perceived in the blink of an eye. Yet even now, the intellect *does* perceive—and if one person sees, it does not mean no one else can. Many sages perceive all that has been described, just as Zayd and ‘Amr see with their eyes.  

Conversely, consider this problem: when Zayd claims to know the realities of the celestial realm, ‘Amr denies it, saying, "How can *you* know when *I* do not?" Poor Zayd has no recourse, for ‘Amr cannot see what he sees, nor can he be taught to recognize Zayd’s knowledge.  

The proof that the intellect knows all existent things is that the intellect is a simple, uncomposed substance—like the celestial spheres and abstract entities. What is said to lie beyond the intellect’s grasp is always composite, and these composites depend on the simple, not the other way around. Thus, the composite fails to apprehend the simple, not the simple the composite.  

An analogy: suppose Zayd builds a house. Out of compassion for ‘Amr, he adds hidden chambers and adorns the walls with beautiful designs. Before even entering, ‘Amr—based on a mere description—says to Zayd, "You do not know what hidden chambers or beautiful designs are in this house!" How, then, can one say that the intellect—which has constructed the very order of existence—does not know? The truth is that one projects his own flaws onto the intellect.  

When it is said that if the soul is wretched, it cannot ascend beyond the sphere of the moon, yet if it is abstract, nothing will be hidden from it—and if it is blessed, reaching the celestial spheres and beyond, nothing in the abstract realm (*malakūt*) will remain concealed—this is a necessary proof. Thus, when the soul, after separating from the body, perceives such realities—realities it cannot know today—the obstruction is the body, not the intellect. This is sound reasoning.  

Therefore, the intellect is aware of all existent things. This much is obligatory in response to this topic.

### Notes:  
1. *Shqī* (wretched) and *sa‘īd* (blessed) refer to the soul’s moral and spiritual state, determining its posthumous ascent or confinement.  
2. *Maḥall* (topic/point of discussion) refers to the specific philosophical question under debate.
