### Summary of the Seventh Epistle on the Soul Concerning Resurrection and the Hereafter  
In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful  

Know that it is incumbent upon the wise to reflect upon their own nature and to recognize the essence of their being. If someone claims to have mastered the sciences of philosophy and religion yet remains ignorant of their own essence, they are utterly false, and no knowledge shall ever be manifest to them. Indeed, they cannot truly comprehend any knowledge. Even if they know something, they remain in doubt and cannot attain its certain truth.  

All that people need pertains either to the body or the soul. What concerns the body is worldly, while what concerns the soul pertains to the hereafter. The wise know that the body is transient and base, destined to return to dust. Therefore, understanding the nature of the soul’s felicity and wretchedness, accumulating felicity, and distancing oneself from wretchedness is far better than being preoccupied with the governance of the perishable and neglecting the universal soul.  

The ancients were never concerned with the felicity or wretchedness of the body; their efforts were entirely devoted to seeking the soul. The felicity of the soul is its ascension to the celestial realm, the heavens, which is the eternal paradise—its union with the world of divine dominion, the soul, and the Necessary Intellect, which is the true Beloved.  

Know that in the teachings of the prophets, sages, and philosophers, and in their scriptures, there is no discourse except that goodness is the felicity of both worlds, and evil is the wretchedness of both worlds. Goodness elevates the soul to eternal felicity, while evil condemns it to eternal wretchedness. This felicity and wretchedness pertain to the hereafter, not the fleeting five days of worldly life. The felicity of the hereafter lies in knowing the essence of the soul.  

We say the soul is a simple, luminous substance—living, life-giving, and luminous by its very nature. Knowledge exists within it potentially while it is bound to the body, but when it actualizes, it becomes intrinsic to its essence. This matter is somewhat complex, and its resolution lies in the agreement that the soul is potentially knowing. Let us take this as a "universal affirmative premise," as when we say every soul is potentially knowing.  

The reason for its knowledge is its orientation toward the Universal Soul, which is like a mirror reflecting all existents. Just as every form appears in a mirror, so too does every existing and future knowledge appear in the Universal Soul. The individual soul, attached to a body like Zayd and governing it, is oriented toward the body while also connected to the Universal Soul. The forms of knowledge appear in it just as they do in the Universal Soul itself.  

An analogy from the sensible world: if a mirror is placed before the moon, a star, or an image, the first mirror and its reflection appear in a second mirror. If a third mirror is placed before the second, whatever is reflected in the second appears in the third. Remarkably, the third and second mirrors, along with their reflections, also appear in the first mirror. This analogy becomes clear upon careful reflection.  

Thus, it is established why the soul is knowing, how its knowledge functions, and why its attachment to the body and the distractions of the senses keep some knowledge potential rather than actual. The reason the soul, while connected to the body, does not fully receive the forms of knowledge from the Universal Soul is the opacity of the body. Many analogies from the sensible world illustrate this.  

For example, if a crystal is left unpolished, it does not reflect light and receives no more illumination than a stone. If one side is polished and placed facing the sun while the unpolished side faces the ground, the polished side receives sunlight, but due to the unpolished side, no reflection appears on the ground, nor can it display what it has received. But if the lower side is also polished, it will reflect the image it has received.  

Similarly, the soul’s lower aspect is attached to the body, and the dullness of the senses prevents it from receiving knowledge. If the senses are refined through the discipline of learning and intellectual pursuits, the forms of knowledge will manifest within it.  

Another analogy: if a clear glass pane is held in the air, the air brightens from its reflection. But if placed against a dark wall, neither the wall brightens nor does it reflect, due to the wall’s opacity. Yet if a hole is made in the wall and the glass is placed within it, the house will brighten from the sun’s reflection.  

Alternatively, if the air were impure, our eyes could never see the sun, moon, or stars. But because the outer layer of air, adjacent to the celestial sphere, is pure, and the layer near us is equally clear, whatever that outer layer receives is cast into our eyes. Though our eyes cannot perceive the true beauty of the moon, sun, and stars as they are, they are not entirely deprived.  

The Universal Soul is nobler than the stars, and our soul is nobler than the air. The faculty of sight perceives what it does only through the soul, for without the soul, how could vision exist? Thus, the Universal Soul is like the stars, the human soul like the air, and the senses like mirrors. Reason understands that if the air can transmit what it receives from the first layer to the second, the rational soul is even more capable.  

By the Necessary Existent, we have not seen or read such analogies in any book before, and we have presented them here in this manner. If fairness is granted, this insight—and others like it—must originate from the soul itself.  

Thus, through these premises, it is established that every soul is knowing, and there is no doubt about its life and luminosity. Therefore, it would be a grave injustice if someone possessing such a soul, when asked, "What is your origin?" or "Where is your return?" remains ignorant—yet still claims mastery of the sciences.  

We say the origin of humanity, given the diversity of beliefs, is disputed. According to the prophets and messengers, the soul’s origin is the Necessary Existent, and it is established in scripture that the Necessary Existent is the Intellect. There are many proofs for this.  

The divine philosophers—such as Plato, Aristotle, Thales, Pythagoras, Socrates, and their like, whose words are authoritative—hold that the human soul is of a simple substance, one that sustains both worlds.  

But Galen the physician claims the soul is a vapor that perishes with the body and does not endure. The great scholars have refuted this in their works. We say Galen’s claim is unreliable because he was a physician, not a philosopher—whereas Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle were both physicians and philosophers. Physicians acknowledge that Socrates was no less skilled in medicine than Galen, yet regarding the soul, he was so extreme as to affirm reincarnation (though he did not specify whether it applied to all or some). Thus, Galen’s argument is refuted, and the philosophers have invalidated his claim through proofs that the soul is a simple substance.  

Since the soul is a simple substance, its return must necessarily be to its original essence. Thus, we say the state of resurrection can only be one of two: felicity or wretchedness, as we have explained in the Epistle on Pain and Pleasure.  

The prophets, however numerous, unanimously affirm the resurrection of bodies and a day when all creation shall return. The divine philosophers agree that felicity or wretchedness pertains to the soul, while the body is perishable. The wise reconcile both views, seeking the truth as the prophets have stated.  

They say that every 36,000 years, the entire universe cycles anew: all earth becomes trees, plants, animals, and minerals, then perishes and returns to dust. Lands where the dead were buried become fields and trees, nourishment for animals, and from that nourishment, the very individuals who had perished are reborn. This transformation is possible even in a thousand years, let alone 36,000. There is no doubt that resurrection means revival, and revival is certain whether it takes a hundred thousand or a thousand thousand years.  

But the resurrection of spirits, without doubt or ambiguity, is firmly established in both scripture and philosophy: every soul shall be resurrected.  

In Arabic, "ba‘th" (resurrection) has three meanings:  
1. "Ba‘atha rasūlan" – to send a messenger.  
2. "Ba‘atha al-nā’im" – to awaken the sleeper.  
3. "Ba‘atha al-mayyit" – to revive the dead.  

All three apply to the soul:  
- First, in sending the soul to the lower world for governance.  
- Second, in awakening it when it separates from the body.  
- Third, in reviving it.  

Thus, it is known that the death of the body is the birth of the soul, and the life of the body is the death of the soul. The resurrection of the soul occurs in an instant when the body dies—as our Prophet (peace be upon him) said: "Whoever dies, their resurrection has come."  

The meaning is that the resurrection of each person occurs at their death. Whatever they have stored in this earth—virtue or ignorance, good or evil—will remain with their soul after the body perishes. But if what the soul has stored is of a substance whose form cannot separate from materiality, and if that form does not align with the higher world—if it is wrathful or lustful—the world of divine dominion will not accept it. It will remain in the lower world, abiding eternally in Hell. This is the true meaning of resurrection.
