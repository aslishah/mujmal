### **Summary of the Fourth Epistle on Psychical Matters Concerning the Intellect and the Intelligible**  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Having established that sensible things are all accidents, and bodies are forms in matter, and that they may be apprehended through sense perception—and since sense perception is a bodily instrument, and whatever is perceived through a bodily instrument is itself more spiritual—then the spiritual must be apprehended by something akin to it. Thus, what is apprehended by the senses is the sensible, not the intelligible.  

We say that the first thing incumbent upon the rational being is to know his own body, for nothing is closer to the rational being than his body. Among people, some have sharper senses and more complete faculties of perception. Thus, we must first understand what perception is and how many kinds there are. In other epistles, we have stated that the senses are hearing, sight, smell, taste, and touch. But within us, there is another faculty of perception besides these senses, and beyond them is imagination, which is like a chamber before the brain. Whatever the sense of sight apprehends is immediately presented to it. If imagination is in its proper place—for a reason we shall mention later—then when an image reaches it, imagination acts upon it. If the cogitative faculty is in its place, it applies itself to contemplate the nature, origin, and state of that image. If the purpose of this is merely the apparent state, the estimative faculty is unconcerned with it. But if the purpose is the true meaning of that form, then estimation intervenes—either to grasp its meaning or not. Here lies a difficulty for the possessor of knowledge, for estimation often presents things that the intellect, after much study, knows to be impossible, yet they remain fixed in the imagination. Or if one denies them, they may be accused of disbelief.  

What estimation presents, the intellect may later attain—such as when estimation says that anything without front and back, left and right, above and below, cannot exist. At first, the intellect has no answer until it acquires its proof. Then it masters the science of astronomy and the structure of the universe, distinguishing between the corporeal and spiritual worlds, knowing that anything with a head, hands, feet, face, and back must have front and back, right and left, above and below. Otherwise, if it lacks these attributes, it does not possess them. Thus, estimation is dismissed, and proof takes its place.  

For example, suppose one forms a sphere, as is customary with globes, and places it before oneself, observing it without its usual instruments—hands, feet, head, face, or back—then one knows that in the corporeal world, there exists something without these six directions. Thus, one concludes that if it is possible for a body to lack these attributes, it is even more so for the soul. Therefore, one realizes that as long as the intellect is not acquainted with logic, premises, and demonstration, estimation will occupy it with sophistry. But once logic is acquired, even if estimation presents something false, the intellect is not helpless; rather, it examines its premises. If they are primary or sensible, it accepts them without objection. If estimation has judged them—whether rightly or wrongly—it entrusts them to memory, which is composed of the brain's subtle faculties.  

This faculty of memory is more refined than the human soul’s faculty, for if it were abstracted from intelligibles or sensibles, it would retain them all. Yet people have another faculty of perception, which each interprets differently. Some say it resides in the rear of the brain, while others say it is a faculty of the celestial soul. This faculty is such that when the imaginative faculty perceives something, this faculty acts upon it, discerning the inner meaning. In appearance, irrational animals share this with humans—like a sparrow recognizing the form of a hawk without ever having seen one, or a lamb instinctively understanding the nature of a wolf it has never encountered. But in humans, this meaning is subtler, suited to human temperament. The philosophers call it the "faculty of the soul," which foretells future events, while others call it "physiognomy" (*firāsa*), by which forms are interpreted. For instance, they say, "This form resembles such-and-such a beast, and thus it will act accordingly." They then recall that action and call it physiognomy—but this is false, for it is merely likening one thing to another, like saying a lion’s eye is red and full of arrogance. When they see the same trait in a human eye, they say the man is courageous—but this is mere analogy. True physiognomy is that which foretells events before they occur. If such foretelling is consistent and without error, the person is called a "soothsayer," which is a lofty rank, for this meaning is above mere physiognomy.  

Thus, this faculty—though some claim it resides in the forepart of the brain—is connected to a royal, intelligible meaning, not a corporeal one. There are other faculties like this: the animal and vegetative faculties, but our discussion concerns this one.  

When the rational being reflects, other things become known to him that cannot be apprehended by these ten senses—such as the soul, its definition, its substance, estimation, the intellect and its attributes, and the unity and majesty of the Necessary Existent. Thus, there are apprehensions beyond the senses, which must reside in something else—either a faculty within the human soul or the human soul itself. Both possibilities are inseparable from the human soul. We have elsewhere established that the human soul is neither a body nor composite; it is a simple substance.  

What we have said—that if imagination or estimation is in its place—applies during states of anger, fear, or desire. For example, if one sees the image of a beloved, even among many others, he is oblivious to all else. If an enemy of the lover or beloved is present, the lover, absorbed in joy, does not see the enemy. Likewise, if one is overcome by anger, though many things are visible, they are all ignored. The same applies to fear.  

Our purpose is to demonstrate that the knower, the known, and knowledge are one—just as the intellect, the intelligible, and the act of intellection are one. Thus, we must first understand what the intellect is, who the intellector is, and what the intelligible is.  

We say the intellect is a faculty within the human soul, for we have elsewhere established that the intellect is a luminous substance brought into existence by the Necessary Existent before all things. It is the First Intention, pure goodness, and the very act of the Necessary Existent, while other things are secondary effects. From the Necessary Existent, the effusion of mercy, grace, and knowledge—and whatever is destined to exist in both worlds—flows perpetually through the intellect’s essence without interruption. Whatever the intellect receives, it receives all at once, like air receiving light from the sun or a room receiving light from a lamp. Thus, what the intellect receives from the Necessary Existent, it bestows upon the soul out of compassion and mercy, in proportion to the soul’s capacity. The soul receives it all at once.  

The intellect has two aspects:  
1. Its luminous essence.  
2. The effusion of knowledge from the Necessary Existent, which is also luminous.  

Thus, the intellect possesses two glories and two powers:  
1. The glory and power of its own substance.  
2. The glory and power from the effusion of the Necessary Existent.  

Likewise, the soul has two glories and two powers:  
1. The glory and power of its own substance.  
2. The glory and power from the effusion of the intellect.  

What the intellect receives from the Necessary Existent, it gives to the soul, and the soul becomes knowing through the intellect. From its own substance and the effusion of knowledge it receives from the intellect, it gives to the entire world. Thus, the more subtle a thing in the world, the better it receives. In the world, nothing is nobler than the stars, then the celestial spheres, then humans. If there is anything else with a refined nature or balanced temperament—like parrots and similar creatures—it also receives some of this power.  

This substance and its effusion are most present in the stars—more in the fixed stars than the planets, more in the higher stars than the lower, more in the lower stars than the moon, and more in the moon than in humans, and more in humans than in anything else on earth. Thus, what is attached to man from the Universal Soul is a substance from the soul’s essence and a power from the intellect’s effusion, which is connected to the soul.  

An analogy in the sensible world would be placing a crystal sphere on the ground, a crystal cup upon it, and drawing a circle of fire around it. The fire projects its light into the cup, and the cup, by its clarity, projects the fire’s light into the crystal. Thus, the cup has two powers: one from its own clarity and one from the fire. The crystal sphere in the middle receives both powers.  

Here, the fire represents the intellect, the cup the Universal Soul, and the crystal sphere man. The intellect is the light of both powers the crystal possesses: one from the soul, the other from the intellect. The example of the fire and the soul is the reverse of the cup. Similarly, if sunlight shines through a glass dome and a person stands inside, the sun represents the intellect, the dome the Universal Soul, and the person man. Thus, man acquires two powers: one from the sun through the dome and the other from the glass’s reflection. The reflection represents the soul, and the sunlight the intellect—this is quite clear.  

Through these premises, we understand what the intellect and soul are. But most people who claim excellence think the intellector is the human being—meaning the human body—because action occurs within it. This is an error, for in Persian, *‘aql* (intellect) means "wisdom," and *nafs* (soul) means "spirit." Thus, it is the human soul that is the intellector by virtue of the intellect’s light and effusion, not the composite human being. For wisdom resides in the spirit, not the body—wisdom is the spirit’s life, just as the intellect is the soul’s life. The Necessary Existent is the Living, the Sustainer of all.  

Thus, we have explained what the intellect is and who the intellector is. As for the intelligible—know that the soul, by virtue of this intellectual faculty within it, is knowing in potentiality, not in actuality. The reason it is not knowing in actuality is that the soul has one aspect facing the body and another facing the celestial realm, the Universal Soul, and the Active Intellect. Due to its connection to the body and the density of matter, the knowledge within the soul does not manifest in humans. When it does manifest, it is in potentiality, not actuality.  

Thus, when one constructs a ladder from sensible things, deducing the unseen from the seen, and apprehends the intelligibles—what exists in the celestial realm, the soul, and the intellect—and distinguishes between intellect and the sensible through demonstrative proof, then this intellect transitions from potentiality to actuality.  

Let us provide an analogy for clarity: An unpolished steel mirror has the potential to reflect anything, but as long as it remains unpolished, it is merely potential. Once polished, with the sun’s aid, it reflects everything, and what was potential becomes actual. Similarly, the human soul, due to its bodily aspect, cannot receive knowledge from the Universal Soul and the Active Intellect because of the body’s density. But through discipline in mathematics, geometry, logic, astronomy, physics, and related sciences, it becomes polished like a mirror, receiving from the Active Intellect and the Universal Soul just as a mirror receives forms. In this state, whatever distracts it becomes its veil.  

Thus, the intellect is this light of the intellect connected through the body, the intellector is the human soul, and the intelligible is the form of these things within the intellect. These three are one—just as knowledge, the knower, and the known are one.  

*And God knows best.*
