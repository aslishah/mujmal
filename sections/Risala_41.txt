### **Summary of All Theological Epistles in One Treatise**  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Know that this treatise presents various schools of thought, as well as matters of belief, disposition, and mutual support. In this treatise, there is no bias, partisanship, or discord. Therefore, we deemed it proper to compile one chapter divided into ten sections, each containing what is fitting from the beliefs, dispositions, and methods of the philosophers, as well as what occupies them from the beginning to the end of their lives. What we have presented in this treatise is entirely free of obscurity, and all that is in this book—which conforms to the beliefs of the philosophers—we have included. As for anything inclined toward other perspectives, we have disregarded it and omitted arguments, for tales and fables are abundant. Truth must prevail.  

### **First Section**  
Know that in these epistles, we have recounted many of the philosophers’ beliefs, though not systematically. Here, we shall summarize them concisely.  

Belief (*iʿtiqād*) is the path upon which people stand and through which they seek their ultimate return (*maʿād*). This is the true meaning of belief, just as opinion (*raʾy*), doctrine (*madhhab*), religion (*dīn*), and sacred law (*sharīʿah*) are. However, the sacred law is the disposition of a sent prophet or messenger, and through religion and law, all that we have mentioned is sought.  

The foundation of this belief lies in correct contemplation (*naẓar*) of the structure of the universe—what is called "existence" (*hast*), from the lowest to the most exalted, from the circumference of the great celestial sphere to the center of the earth. This contemplation cannot be achieved unless one first casts aside all preconceived beliefs and purifies oneself of all opinions. If the seeker of knowledge is capable, well and good; otherwise, they must seek a trustworthy physician-teacher, submit themselves to him, and allow him to examine all their major and minor faculties, determining which of the four humors dominates—especially black bile (*sawdāʾ*) and burnt blood, which the learned physicians call *melancholia*—so that, with the physician’s skilled assistance, the imbalance may be corrected.  

Then, one must withdraw from society for a month or less, devoting oneself to bodily discipline, ensuring that no trace of anger or desire remains, and making peace within oneself. All religious and philosophical beliefs must be cast aside. Only when one has attained this state will their contemplation be sound. If they wish to perceive the truth, the truth will reveal itself to them. Contemplation cannot be achieved except through this method, and it is a path with no equal.  

If, in their time, they find a teacher who embodies the qualities we have described, they must attach themselves to him and learn from him the path of origin (*mabdaʾ*) and return (*maʿād*). This teacher must be virtuous in appearance and character, humble, and knowledgeable in all sciences—both those pertaining to sacred law and those pertaining to philosophy. He must be free of hostility, avoid harming others, and live in seclusion. If someone wrongs him, he repays evil with good and does not retaliate. He must have no attachment to worldly accumulation, shun lust, avoid entanglement in family and worldly affairs, and accept nothing from people on account of his knowledge—except, if necessary, the bare minimum for sustenance.  

Such a man is complete, needing nothing from the world. He does not teach the unworthy, nor withhold knowledge from the deserving. He neither speaks nor listens to ill words. If these qualities are found in a person, he may be taken as a teacher. If the seeker has undergone spiritual discipline (*riyāḍah*) and finds such a teacher, they may rely on him, for with such a guide, further discipline is unnecessary. But if no teacher is found, discipline is essential, for every dominant humor inclines a person toward a corresponding belief—and it is futile to argue with such a person.  

The discipline we have prescribed is exceedingly brief. Indeed, it is well-known through continuous reports (*khabar mutawātir*) how the Greeks acquired knowledge. Their minimal discipline involved gradually reducing their food intake—diminishing their daily ration by three *dirhams* until they reached thirty, then maintaining that for a month, then reducing it by one *dirham* daily until they reached ten. Their food consisted of boiled chickpeas with almond oil. For forty days, they subsisted on ten *dirhams* daily, and during this period, they deduced all the sciences they sought—Euclidean geometry, arithmetic, music, talismans, the properties of minerals, and other sciences—on which scholars agree as their deductions. Compared to their discipline, what we have mentioned is nothing.  

After all this, contemplation must be sound and free from bias. When one contemplates, they must first examine the instruments of logic, then arithmetic, geometry, and astronomy, seeking to understand the structure of the higher and lower worlds and grasping their true nature. Then they must proceed to the philosophical sciences, discerning the causes of things, investigating origins and ends, and refraining from forming beliefs until the truth reveals itself. The divine philosophers have affirmed that such a belief is the sure means of salvation.  

Thus, we say: Know that contemplation is of oneself and of what is beyond oneself. When one contemplates oneself, they perceive a body (*tan*) and a soul (*jān*) attached to it. We have given many proofs that the body is of the earthly realm, while the soul is of the realm of spirits. The body was nonexistent, then came into being, and the soul—if fairness is granted—was likewise nonexistent, then came into being. For if it had existed before, upon self-awareness, one would know where and how it had been.  

Through many proofs, one realizes that they cannot perceive their own origin except by recognizing that the soul’s existence is bound to the body. We have said that the human being is a microcosm (*ʿālam ṣaghīr*), and the universe is a macrocosm (*ʿālam kabīr*). Thus, the body’s existence mirrors the universe’s. Both are contingent (*muḥdath*), and no contingent exists without a cause (*muḥdith*). The cause of the universe is its Maker (*ṣāniʿ*). Whoever says the universe has a Maker must acknowledge the existence of the made (*maṣnūʿ*), just as writing implies a writer. To claim that a universe of such grandeur, with its wonders, exists without a Maker is like claiming a book or knowledge exists without a writer—which is pure absurdity.  

Thus, know—as we have said in earlier epistles—that nothing existed except the Necessary Existent (*wājib al-wujūd*), whose necessity is intrinsic, not derived from another. All other existents are intrinsically possible (*mumkin al-wujūd*), for their cause is the Necessary Existent, whose cause is itself.  

One may say that two comes from one, but not that one comes from something, nor that another preceded one. All numbers to infinity derive from one, which is eternal. One precedes all, and nothing precedes one. Thus, all existents other than the Necessary Existent came into being, while the Necessary Existent has always been, without a first.  

Just as numbers are infinite and all derive from one—which has no end—so too all existents derive from the Necessary Existent. Existence has no end, and the Necessary Existent is unbound. As one sustains all numbers, the Necessary Existent sustains all existents. If this seems difficult, it may be deduced from the premises we have mentioned, requiring no further judgment.  

As for the differences among philosophical schools, know that their disagreements are not in principles (*uṣūl*). If they differ in particulars (*furūʿ*), it is not a divisive difference. For them, the principle is knowing the Necessary Existent, while particulars concern the structure of the universe.  

Plato, in recognizing the Necessary Existent, articulated a unity (*tawḥīd*) that remains unparalleled among the learned. He revealed the causes of sciences pertaining to principles and particulars as no one else has. In particulars, he spoke in a way that does not perish, offering two demonstrative syllogisms (*qiyās burhānī*) on generation and corruption. He argued that the Necessary Existent is not compelled to act, for It needs nothing. What It does is pure grace (*jawd maḥḍ*), not obligation.  

The impossibility of necessary action for the Necessary Existent arises from three aspects:  
1. **Natural Action** (e.g., fire burning, air moving, water flowing). The Necessary Existent does not act thus, for celestial spheres act by specific properties (*khāṣṣiyyah*), not nature (*ṭabʿ*).  
2. **Compulsion by Another** (e.g., a master commanding a servant, a king his subjects, a father his child, a teacher his student). The Necessary Existent is the cause of all, with none preceding to compel It.  
3. **Need for the Act** (e.g., a teacher needing a student, a laborer needing wages). The Necessary Existent has no need; Its acts are not out of necessity.  

Other motives fall under these three. Thus, the Necessary Existent’s acts are neither natural, compensatory, nor compelled—only pure mercy (*raḥmah*). This is a conditional disjunctive syllogism (*qiyās sharṭī munfaṣil*).  

We say the universe’s structure has two possibilities:  
1. It coexists eternally (*maʿa maʿa*) with the Necessary Existent—but this would mean the universe is the Necessary Existent, which is impossible.  
2. It comes into being after a determined time—but this implies temporal will (*irādah*), which cannot apply to the Necessary Existent.  

We shall elaborate on this in the philosophers’ creed.
