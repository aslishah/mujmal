### Summary of the Twelfth Epistle on Hermeneutics in Logic  
In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  

We stated in the previous epistle that speech consists of nouns, verbs, and particles. A *noun* denotes a thing without reference to time—indeed, the essence of the thing is known through the noun. As for the *verb*, logicians call it a "word" (*kalima*), and it denotes both a thing and the time of that thing. The *particle* is called a "connector" (*rābiṭa*) or an "adverb" (*adāt*). Some logicians hold that nouns and verbs are complete in themselves and that the meaning of particles cannot be understood without them. In any case, since no harm or fallacy arises from particles, it is better that they exist. Examples of nouns include Zayd, ‘Amr, tree, and stone; verbs include "did," "struck," "went," and the like; and particles include "in," "on," and "to," as in the phrase "Zayd went on." If one says, "Zayd went," it conveys meaning, though incompletely. Often, without particles, no meaning is conveyed at all, as in "Zayd [is] house," from which no clear meaning emerges, for it is unknown whether Zayd is in the house or has built a house—and many such examples exist.  
-131-  
Thus, nouns, verbs, and particles must be used together, and the composition of speech is based on this structure. When speech is composed, it is called a *statement* (*qawl*), a *proposition* (*qaḍiyya*), or a *decisive utterance* (*sukhan-i jazm*). When it involves affirmation or negation, it is called a *report* (*khabar*), which may sometimes be false and sometimes true. Among these terms, the most well-known among logicians is *proposition* (*qaḍiyya*), which is a statement upon which a judgment is made, such as saying, "Such-and-such is thus," or "Such-and-such is not thus." This "is" and "is not" are called the *judgment* (*ḥukm*), as when we say, "Fire is hot," or "Fire is not hot." The judgment that takes the form of "is" is called *affirmative* (*mūjib*), and the judgment that takes the form of "is not" is called *negative* (*sālib*). These judgments may sometimes be true and sometimes false, as when we say, "Fire is not hot," which is false.  

Propositions are of two types: one is called an *absolute judgment* (*ḥukm-i muṭlaq*), such as "Fire is burning," and the other is called a *conditional judgment* (*sharṭī*), such as saying, "If fire is hot, it burns." The conditional is further divided into two kinds: one is called *connected* (*muttasil*), and the other *disjunctive* (*munfaṣil*), as in saying, "This statement is either true or false."  

Propositions also vary in their quantifiers: some are universal, and others are particular. A universal proposition is one like, "All humans are rational," while a particular proposition is like, "Some humans are scribes." A universal affirmative judgment is called *universal affirmative* (*kullī mūjib*), and a universal negative judgment is called *universal negative* (*kullī sālib*). A particular affirmative judgment, such as "Some humans are scribes," is called *particular affirmative* (*juzʾī mūjib*), while a particular negative judgment is called *particular negative* (*juzʾī sālib*). These four types of propositions are called *categorical* (*ḥamlī*), and they are so named because they consist of two parts: one called the *subject* (*mawḍūʿ*) and the other the *predicate* (*maḥmūl*). For example, in the statement "Fire is burning," "fire" is the subject, "burning" is the predicate, and "is" is the judgment. However, the proposition we have given is called *indefinite* (*muhmal*), because we did not make a universal judgment by saying "all fire" or "everything that is fire is burning"—we omitted the terms "all" (*kull*) and "every" (*kullu*). Thus, this proposition is called *indefinite* (*muhmal*), and in debate and demonstration, it is treated as a particular judgment, not a universal one.  

The terms we mentioned—"every" (*kull*), "all" (*jamīʿ*), or "whatever" (*kullu mā*)—are called *quantifiers* (*sūr*). Quantifiers are either universal or particular. Universal quantifiers are either negative or affirmative, and in reality, there are two types: affirmative, as in "All humans are animals," and negative, as in "No human is an animal." Thus, "all" and "no" are expressions of *universal quantifiers* (*sūr-i kullī*), and in place of "all," one may use "every" or "whatever." However, no other term substitutes for "no." If the quantifier is particular, as in "Some humans are not scribes," this "some" is called a *particular negative quantifier* (*sūr-i juzʾī sālib*). It is not permissible to make a judgment about a single individual and then generalize it universally, as in saying, "Zayd is just, therefore all humans are just," for it does not necessarily follow that if Zayd is just, all humans must be just. Or one might say, "Zayd is not just, therefore no human is just." Such propositions are called *singular* (*shakhṣiyya*) or *specific* (*makhṣūṣa*).  

In categorical and conditional propositions, fallacies such as *equivocation* (*muʿaddala*) and *indefiniteness* (*muhmala*) may occur, and one must guard against them. The propositions most useful in demonstration are four: two affirmative and two negative—universal affirmative, universal negative, particular affirmative, and particular negative, as previously mentioned.
