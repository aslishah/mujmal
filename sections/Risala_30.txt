### Summary of the Fifteenth Epistle on Natural Sciences Concerning the Nature of Pain and Pleasure  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Many among the wise harbor an aversion to the death of their bodies, despite knowing that eternal comfort and everlasting life lie elsewhere, and that there is no true benefit in the animal state. For the wise, this aversion arises from the weakness of the soul. The example of such a wise person who feels this aversion is like that of a child whose father entrusts him to a schoolteacher, instructing the teacher: *"Over this period, you must teach this child all that a servant of the king ought to know, instill in him intellectual and physical disciplines until he becomes a master."* Yet the child, out of love for play and amusement and unwillingness to endure the hardship of learning, daily seeks to appease the teacher—secretly bringing sweets from home, seeking beautiful beloveds for the teacher, knowing the teacher’s inclination toward such things. When the teacher indulges in these daily, the child too becomes engrossed in eating, copulation, and play, growing accustomed to them. As time passes, the teacher falls ill from excessive indulgence and lies on his deathbed. The child, knowing he has no other refuge but to return to his parents’ home, fears his father’s reproach and wishes the teacher would not die, lest he be shamed for his ignorance.  

Thus, the five senses are the teacher, the rational soul is the child, the universal intellect and soul are the father and mother, and the sweetness, pleasures, and beloveds are the worldly delights. The rational soul ought to attain intelligible truths through the senses and the common sense (which is its teacher), perform good deeds and acts of worship, and gather provisions for the hereafter. But if it does the opposite, it necessarily develops an aversion to death. And when the rational soul becomes habituated to lust and fails to do good, even if it possesses the strength to ascend to the higher world after parting from the body, it will shrink in shame and never reach that realm to behold its true parents—the universal soul and intellect.  

We say that everything in the universe, from the center of the earth to the outermost sphere, experiences pleasure and pain according to its nature. Since pleasure and pain are expressions arising from language, we begin with animals and say that the being in whom pain and pleasure are most evident is man, the noblest of animals, possessing the most refined senses. He is composed of soul and body, and his soul experiences pleasures—while their opposites are pains. The root of all pleasures is fourfold: appetitive, animal, human, and spiritual.  

Appetitive pleasure is the natural enjoyment of food agreeable to one’s temperament. Animal sensory pleasure is of two kinds: first, copulation at the desired time and place; second, wrath—whether just or unjust—in taking vengeance upon another. Human intellectual pleasure is the delight attained upon grasping intelligible truths, which is the essence of knowing a thing. Spiritual, angelic pleasure is that which reaches the soul after parting from the body. Pleasure, lust, and wrath are shared between man and animal, just as animal and intellectual pleasures are shared between man and angel. The spiritual is exclusive to souls that have parted from the body.  

As for plants, the Law (*sharīʿa*) speaks of their pleasure in the verse: *"And they are fearful in awe of Him"* (Qur’an 21:28), though they certainly feel no pain. Non-rational animals experience both pain and pleasure, but their pleasures are entirely bodily. Man experiences both bodily and spiritual pleasures. Every human pleasure is of two kinds: one mediated by the body, the other exclusive to the soul.  

The bodily pleasures are sevenfold:  
1. Perception through the eye—the beauty of colors, forms, patterns, and natural figures.  
2. Through hearing—pleasant sounds, song, melody, praise, and the like.  
3. Through taste—agreeable foods.  
4. Through touch—such as softness and roughness.  
5. Through smell—what suits one’s temperament and brings comfort to the brain.  
6. The pleasure of copulation—union with the desired one.  
7. The pleasure of vengeance upon an enemy.  

All these reach the soul through the body’s mediation, and each pleasure is twofold: first, when the desire is fulfilled; second, when it is remembered. For example, when one beholds a beautiful face, a pleasure reaches the soul—distinct from the fulfillment of desire. This is why Paradise and Hell are immediate realities for them: fulfillment is Paradise, and deprivation is Hell, which brings aversion to the soul. In truth, the soul’s real pleasure lies in its ascent to the heavens and celestial spheres—this is eternal Paradise, the fulfillment of desire. Hell is remaining in the earthly world—this is deprivation. Hence, the sages condemn wrath and harming others but do not censure lust as severely, for speaking openly of lust is improper—some matters are best left unsaid, and others cannot be recounted here due to length.  

As for spiritual pleasures exclusive to the soul, they are fourfold:  
1. When it conceives intelligible truths, immense joy arises—sometimes even in sensory matters, as when a geometer delights in a geometric form or a scholar attains knowledge.  
2. When it acquires sound beliefs, storing provisions for the hereafter.  
3. When it beholds in itself a praiseworthy character approved by the intellect.  
4. When it is praised for good knowledge, deeds, or virtues—as when a prophet is lauded for his prophethood or a consummate poet for his eloquence.  

We say that in the universe, pain and pleasure are twofold: one for the body, the other for the soul. The body’s share is in food, drink, clothing, fragrance (which is air), touch (though it may seem devoid of pleasure, its seat is in blood or impurity), and hearing (a fleeting pleasure, for when it ends, aversion follows—its joy lay in its cessation). Visual pleasure is a thousand times worse than auditory, for if present, it brings obsession; if absent, the agony of separation.  

The second kind is the soul’s pleasure, which is also twofold:  
1. Worldly—such as mastery of medicine, geometry, astronomy, arithmetic, or virtues like generosity, courage, and justice.  
2. Otherworldly—ascending to the heavens, celestial spheres, and spirits, witnessing the Necessary Being, abiding eternally in whatever state it desires, and issuing decrees in the earthly realm as the angelic spirits do.  

The pain of body and soul is the opposite of what we have described.  

*And God knows best what is correct.*
