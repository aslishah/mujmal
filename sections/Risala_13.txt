### Summary of the Thirteenth Epistle on the First Analytics in Logic  
In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  

Know that when propositions are used in syllogism, they are called "premises," and when two premises come together, a judgment necessarily follows from them, which is called the "conclusion." An example of this is when it is said: "Every human is an animal, and every animal is a substance." The conclusion follows that "Every human is a substance." Thus, in these two premises, there are three terms: "human," "animal," and "substance," each of which is called a "term." "Human" is called the "minor term," "animal" is called the "middle term" and is also referred to as the "shared term," and "substance" is called the "major term." The middle term is "shared" and is the cause of the "conclusion," for if there were no sharing, there would be no conclusion, and if it were present incorrectly, the conclusion would not be valid. We shall demonstrate through converse and contradiction which premises and conclusions are true and which are false.  

When these three terms come together, the middle term must either be:  
1. The "subject" in one premise and the "predicate" in the other,  
2. The "subject" in both premises, or  
3. The "predicate" in both premises.  

- If in the first premise it is the predicate and in the second the subject, it is taken from the first figure of syllogisms, provided we remember.  
- If it is the predicate in both, it is taken from the second figure.  
- If it is the subject in both, it is taken from the third figure.  

**First Figure**: For example, if it is said: "Every human is an animal, and every animal is mobile," the conclusion follows: "Every human is mobile," and this is of the first mood.  

**Second Figure**: If the middle term is the predicate in both premises, as in: "Every body is created, and no eternal thing is created," the conclusion follows: "No body is eternal."  

**Third Figure**: If the middle term is the subject in both premises, as in: "Every animal is mobile, and some animals are human," the conclusion follows: "Some humans are mobile."  

These premises and terms may sometimes be true and sometimes false. For example, if it is said: "Every animal is a body, and every stone is a body," the conclusion would be: "Some animals are stones." Here, the premises are true, but the conclusion is false. This error arises for two reasons:  
1. This syllogism is of the second figure, and in the second figure, no universal affirmative conclusion is valid. It is necessary that one premise be affirmative and the other negative, but here both are affirmative, and the conclusion is affirmative.  
2. If it is said: "Every rational being is discerning, and no discerning being commits sin," the conclusion would be: "No rational being commits sin," which is false. Both the premises and the conclusion are deceptive, and the falsity of this deception can be shown through contradiction and conversion.  

For instance, the statement "Every rational being is discerning" can be refuted by saying: "Not every rational being is discerning, for the lover, the greedy, the afflicted, and the like are rational but not discerning." Likewise, the statement "No discerning being commits sin" is incorrect because its converse would be: "No sinner is discerning," which is untrue, for prophets and philosophers committed no sin yet were all discerning. Thus, the way to recognize this error is through contradiction and conversion.

### Chapter on the Contradiction of Propositions  
Every proposition that contains an error, when examined by a rational person, will yield another proposition opposed to it, called the "contradiction" of the first proposition. For example, the statement "Every rational being is discerning" is contradicted by "Not every rational being is discerning," thereby invalidating the first proposition.  

The conditions for contradiction are:  
- One must be universal, the other particular.  
- One must be affirmative, the other negative.  
- The subject, predicate, time, place, potentiality, actuality, and entirety or part must be the same in both propositions.  

When these conditions are met, the contradiction is undoubtedly valid.  

### Chapter on the Conversion of Premises  
Conversion is when the subject is made the predicate and the predicate the subject, while the meaning remains unchanged. For example, if it is said: "Every human is speaking," where "human" is the subject and "speaking" is the predicate, then in conversion, "speaking" becomes the subject and "human" the predicate, as in: "Every speaking being is human," preserving the same meaning.  

In connected propositions, such as "If the sun rises, it is day," the converse would be: "If it is day, the sun has risen." In disjunctive propositions, such as "Every number is either even or odd," the converse would be: "Every number is either odd or even."  

In conversion, careful attention is required. The conversion of a universal affirmative proposition (e.g., "Every speaking being is human") retains its meaning if the predicate becomes the subject and the subject the predicate, provided it pertains to the differential or property, and the universal remains universal.  

For example:  
- **Differential**: "Every human is speaking" converts to "Every speaking being is human."  
- **Property**: "Every human is laughing" converts to "Every laughing being is human."  

For propositions that are neither differential nor property, such as "A human is an animal," the conversion yields: "Some animals are human," which is a particular affirmative.  

The conversion of a universal negative remains a universal negative without doubt (e.g., "No human is a stone" converts to "No stone is human"). The conversion of a particular affirmative remains a particular affirmative, but a particular negative has no valid conversion.

### Summary of the Thirteenth Epistle on the Primary Analytics in Logic  
In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful  

Know that when propositions are employed in syllogistic reasoning, they are termed "premises" (muqaddimah). When two premises are conjoined, a necessary judgment follows, which is called the "conclusion" (natījah). For example, if one states: "Every human is an animal, and every animal is a substance," the conclusion follows: "Every human is a substance." In these two premises, there exist three terms (ḥadd): "human," "animal," and "substance."  

These terms are classified thus:  
- "Human" is the minor term (ḥadd aṣghar)  
- "Animal" is the middle term (ḥadd awsaṭ), also called the "shared term" (mushārak)  
- "Substance" is the major term (ḥadd akbar)  

The middle term functions as the cause ('illa) of the conclusion, for without its shared attribution, no valid conclusion would obtain. We shall demonstrate through contraposition (naqīḍ) and conversion ('aks) how to distinguish true premises and conclusions from false ones.  

When these three terms combine, the middle term must necessarily occupy one of three positions:  
1. Subject (mawḍū') in one premise and predicate (maḥmūl) in the other  
2. Subject in both premises  
3. Predicate in both premises  

- **First Figure**: When the middle term is predicate in the minor premise and subject in the major premise (e.g., "Every human is an animal / Every animal is mobile" → "Every human is mobile").  
- **Second Figure**: When the middle term is predicate in both premises (e.g., "Every body is contingent / No eternal being is contingent" → "No body is eternal").  
- **Third Figure**: When the middle term is subject in both premises (e.g., "Every animal is mobile / Some animals are human" → "Some humans are mobile").  

Premises may yield true or false conclusions. For instance:  
- True premises, false conclusion: "Every animal is corporeal / Every stone is corporeal" → "Some animals are stones."  
This error occurs because:  
1) The syllogism improperly applies the second figure (requiring one affirmative and one negative premise)  
2) Deceptive premises like "Every rational being is discerning / No discerning being sins" → "No rational being sins" are falsified through contraposition:  
   - Contraposition of "Every rational being is discerning" yields "Not all rational beings are discerning" (e.g., lovers, the greedy).  
   - Conversion of "No discerning being sins" becomes "No sinner is discerning" – demonstrably false, as prophets were sinless yet discerning.

### Chapter on the Contraposition of Propositions  
Every erroneous proposition, when examined rationally, yields its contradictory (naqīḍ). For example, "Every rational being is discerning" is contradicted by "Not every rational being is discerning," thereby nullifying the original claim.  

Conditions for valid contraposition:  
1. One universal (kullī), one particular (juz'ī)  
2. One affirmative (mūjib), one negative (sālib)  
3. Identity of subject, predicate, temporal/spatial context, potentiality/actuality, and whole/part relationships  

When these conditions are satisfied, the contraposition is incontrovertibly valid.  

### Chapter on the Conversion of Premises  
Conversion ('aks) entails transposing subject and predicate while preserving meaning. For example:  
- Original: "Every human is speaking" (subject: human, predicate: speaking)  
- Converted: "Every speaking being is human"  

Types of conversion:  
1. **Connected conditional**: "If the sun rises, it is day" → "If it is day, the sun has risen."  
2. **Disjunctive**: "Every number is either even or odd" → "Every number is either odd or even."  

Rules for conversion:  
- **Universal affirmative** (kullī mūjib): Converts only when the predicate constitutes a differential (faṣl) or property (khāṣṣah):  
  - Differential: "Every human is speaking" → "Every speaking being is human"  
  - Property: "Every human laughs" → "Every laughing being is human"  
  - Otherwise yields particular: "Humans are animals" → "Some animals are humans"  
- **Universal negative** (kullī sālib): Converts universally ("No human is stone" → "No stone is human")  
- **Particular affirmative** (juz'ī mūjib): Converts particularly  
- **Particular negative**: No valid conversion

### Chapter on Syllogistic Figures  
**First Figure Conditions**:  
1. Middle term is predicate in minor premise, subject in major premise  
2. Minor premise is affirmative  
3. Major premise is universal  

**Second Figure Conditions**:  
1. One affirmative, one negative premise  
2. Major premise universal  
3. Conclusion always negative  

**Third Figure Conditions**:  
1. One universal premise  
2. Minor premise affirmative  
3. Conclusion always particular  

General Rules:  
- No valid syllogism from:  
  - Two negative premises  
  - Two particular premises  
  - Negative minor + particular major  
- Truth-value relations:  
  - True premises → False conclusion  
  - True conclusion → False premise(s)
