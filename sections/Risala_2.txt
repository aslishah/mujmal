### Summary of the Second Epistle on Geometry  
In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  
We say that geometry is of two kinds: intellectual and sensory. As for the *sensory*, it is the knowledge of magnitudes and what pertains to them—geometric shapes—which may be perceived either by the sense of sight or the sense of touch. Magnitudes are of three kinds: line, surface, and body. As for the *intellectual*, it is the knowledge of the dimensions of these magnitudes and what pertains to them, conceived in the soul through thought, in contrast to sensory perception in apprehension and comprehension. Dimensions are also of three kinds: length, width, and depth—these being the attributes of sensory magnitudes. Contemplation of abstract dimensions can only be achieved through the power of thought and the discipline of the soul.  

Sensory geometry, however, is closer to the understanding of the learner, and whatever is grasped prior to practice is a kind of geometry. An example of sensory geometry is length, width, and depth—such as a cube, which has six equal sides, perceivable by the senses, with right and left, front and back, and above and below. This is purely sensory.  

Thus, we say that the origin of a line is a point, as we mentioned in the first epistle that "one" is the origin of number. Here, we mean the *sensory* point, not the intellectual one.

When sensory points are arranged in this manner... a line emerges, and the line is the origin of the surface—just as the point is the origin of the line—and the surface is the origin of the body. A line has length alone, a surface has length and width but no depth. Thus, when a surface is duplicated, the first body is formed.

The purpose of this epistle is to demonstrate that the foundation of all sciences and practices—especially those pertaining to wisdom—is arithmetic and geometry. It is forbidden for anyone to seek knowledge, wisdom, or the recognition of God Almighty without mastering these two sciences.  

We affirm that whoever does not fully grasp these two sciences cannot know God Almighty—whatever they say is error, for divine knowledge comes after natural science and spiritual knowledge.  

Geometry has two aspects: one in the sensory world and one in the intellectual world. How can one who has not first learned the sensory aspect attain the spiritual and intellectual aspect? Whoever claims otherwise is mistaken.  

Know that arithmetic and geometry are the ladder by which one may ascend to divine knowledge and abstract intelligibles. What we have described in arithmetic and geometry can, once mastered, allow the disciplined mind to abstract numbers and shapes from matter and conceive of them purely.  

Once this is achieved, it becomes clear that the forms of all sensible things can be abstracted from sensory perception—just as God (exalted is He) is free from form, as we have shown with the example of the cubic square, whose form appears in the senses while its essence transcends them.  

We have not elaborated extensively in this epistle, for arithmetic and geometry cannot be fully covered here. The purpose is to awaken the intellect, provoke inquiry, ignite the desire for learning, and point the way—not to teach geometry itself.  

For when the wise reflect that what is near them in the senses can be abstracted, they realize that the faculty performing this abstraction must itself be immaterial—and that is the soul. Thus, they yearn for their return [to the spiritual realm], seeking deliverance from the material world—the realm of generation and corruption, the greater Hell—and strive to ascend to the world of spirits, the eternal Paradise, preparing provisions for the journey.  

This suffices on the matter.  
And God is the source of protection and success.
