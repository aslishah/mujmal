### **Summary of the Third Epistle on Physics Concerning Generation and Corruption**  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Know that the bodies beneath the lunar sphere are seven in number, each constituting a *genus* (jins). The philosophers refer to them as the *mothers* (ummahāt) and the *generated beings* (mawālīd). Four of these are fire, air, water, and earth, while the remaining three are minerals, plants, and animals. The four elements are called the *mothers*, each being a distinct genus and substance (jawhar), with no second to it—as we have previously stated that a genus may be said of that which is singular in its essence, just as we have said of the sun. Thus, the four elements may be considered *specific genera*. Animals, plants, and minerals are referred to as *particular generated beings*, while the celestial spheres and heavens are named the *fathers*.  

Now we say that the elements are composed of matter (hayūlā) and form (ṣūrah). Their *matter* is the corporeal body we perceive, and their *form* is what distinguishes them from one another—such as the radiance, light, dryness, and heat of fire; the moisture and coldness of water; the warmth and humidity of air; and the coldness and dryness of earth. Air, water, and earth receive form accidentally, though not permanently. For instance, water may become warm, but only accidentally, and earth may become warm—as with salt, alum, and the like—through transformation, only to return to its original state. Similarly, coldness in air is accidental, as is dryness and solidification in water.  

Forms are of two kinds: one is called the *constitutive form* (ṣūrah muqawwimah), and the other the *complementary form* (ṣūrah mutammimah). The *constitutive form* is that which, if separated, would annihilate the thing, while the *complementary form* is that whose separation does not negate the thing’s existence. An example of a *constitutive form* is the boiling of fire, while its *complementary form* is the heat that follows it. The *simple substance* (jawhar basīṭ) of a body is upheld by its constitutive form, whereas its *complementary form* consists of length, width, and depth—though some may call these its *essential differentia* (faṣl dhātī), since the body cannot exist without them. Later scholars, whose insights into the sciences are more precise, say that length, width, and depth are the body’s *specific properties* (khāṣṣah) and *necessary accidents* (‘araḍ lāzim), like laughter in humans or the blackness of tar and the whiteness of camphor. The *complementary form* follows the *constitutive form*—just as the constitutive form of fire is motion, while its complementary form is heat, with heat following motion and dryness following heat. The same applies to the other elemental forces.  

Now, the *generation* (kawn) of the elements arises from matter and form. For instance, fire is generated from the heat of the celestial sphere, with air serving as its matter and the motion of the sphere as its efficient cause. When fire is extinguished and its *constitutive form* is negated, it becomes air. When air cools due to the sun’s distance, it undergoes transformation into water and descends to the center of water. If the *complementary form* of water—its moisture—is negated over long periods, it crystallizes into quartz or ruby. If its moisture is negated due to earthy particles, substances like mercury, salt, alum, or vitriol emerge. If the earthy particles suspended in water are exceedingly refined and remain in a place where the sun passes directly overhead for extended periods, rubies form. If Mars shares the sun’s zenith, garnets arise; if Mars is in quadrature with the sun, red salt appears; if Venus is present, crystal forms; if Jupiter is at the zenith with Saturn in sextile or trine, diamonds emerge; and if Mercury shares the zenith, emeralds are produced. In summary, whatever potential becomes actual does so through the motions of the stars.  

The *generation* of plants occurs in two ways: either from seed or through spontaneous growth. The causes of plants from seed are water, fertile earth, and the motion of the stars, while the causes of spontaneous growth are water, earth, and the sun’s motion. Indeed, the sun is the cause of animals, plants, and minerals, with no other body sharing its role—though the celestial sphere, stars, and elements are its partners. However, the primary motion, which belongs to the *simple substance*, is the root of all.  

As for *corruption* (fasād), it is the alteration of form, whether for the better or worse. For example, as we have said, salt is composed of water and earth. When its form changes—which is called *corruption*—its moisture returns to the center of air (which is nobler than salt), while its earthy particles remain. Indeed, we have observed with our own eyes that salt, when heated, becomes air. Thus, this is nobler than *generation*, for the earthy particles in salt, aided by the firmly bound moisture, transform into a refined, transparent air. The same applies by analogy to other bodies.  

If *corruption* occurs, it pertains to form, for the *simple substance* and matter are not subject to corruption. This suffices for understanding generation and corruption. *And God knows best what is intended.*
