Here is the English translation of the selected passage from the *Epistles of the Brethren of Purity* (Ikhwān al-Ṣafā), maintaining its scholarly tone and philosophical nuances:

### Summary of the Ninth Epistle on Natural Sciences Concerning the Senses and the Sensed  

**In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful**  

Know that human knowledge of existent beings is acquired through three means:  

1. **The first** is through the *five senses*, which is the primary means of acquiring knowledge. In this, humans share with children and certain animals.  
2. **The second** is through *intellect* by means of primary premises. Here, humans do not share with children or non-rational animals, though the learned and the unlearned both partake in this.  
3. **The third** is through *demonstration*, which is exclusive to philosophers. Knowledge by this means comes after mastering the sciences of mathematics, geometry, and logic—each of which we have discussed in its proper place.  

Sensible objects are perceived through the senses, primary intelligibles through the abstract intellect, and demonstrative knowledge through the instrument of reason, which is logic.  

The senses are corporeal instruments: the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and hand—all functioning in conjunction with the body. However, the faculty of sensation is a spiritual (or "psychic") power, and these faculties perform specific actions in each organ.  

Sensible objects are accidents subsisting in bodies or composite substances (sometimes referred to simply as "body" or "composite substance"). These accidents affect the five senses in terms of quantity and quality.  

- The faculty of vision operates through seven layers and three humors, as we have previously explained.  
- The faculty of hearing resides within the ear, in a membrane stretched across it.  
- The faculty of smell is located in a hollow nerve within the nose, resembling two nipples, its cavity like a honeycomb. Sometimes, when old bones (such as those of oxen or mules) are broken, cavities appear within them resembling the cavities of the olfactory organ.  
- The faculty of taste lies in the moisture spread over the tongue.  
- The faculty of touch is distributed throughout the body but is most potent in the hands.  

Sensible objects are of five kinds:  

1. **Tangibles**, perceived by touch, are of ten types: heat, cold, dryness, moisture, roughness, smoothness, hardness, softness, lightness, and heaviness.  
2. **Tastes**, perceived by the tongue, are of nine kinds: sweetness, sourness, bitterness, saltiness, fattiness, pungency, pleasantness, astringency, and acidity.  
3. **Odors**, perceived by smell, are of two kinds: pleasant and unpleasant.  
4. **Sounds**, perceived by hearing, are of two kinds: animate and inanimate. The inanimate is further divided into natural (e.g., the sound of stone, iron, wood, thunder, wind) and artificial (e.g., drums, horns, flutes, silk). The animate is either rational (human speech) or non-rational (animal sounds). Rational speech is itself of two kinds: demonstrative and non-demonstrative.  
5. **Visuals**, perceived by sight, are of ten types: light, darkness, color, body, surface, form, position, distance, motion, and rest.  

The faculty of touch perceives heat and cold such that when a warm object is touched, natural heat increases in the touch and overcomes the cold within it, allowing the soul to perceive it. Conversely, when a cold object is touched, the natural coldness of the animal increases until it overcomes the innate heat of the human, so the imaginative faculty in the brain perceives it. If the object’s temperature were in equilibrium with the human body, no sensation would occur. However, it is rarely absent in two aspects: either the object is rougher or smoother than the human body, and thus sensation perceives it. If it matches the body in texture—whether of the same kind (like a child or a servant) or different (like a friend, brother, or kin)—then if it is an object of desire, the organs of desire are stimulated; otherwise, no sensation occurs.  

Similarly, hardness is perceived when an object resists the body’s softness, and the psychic faculty in the brain discerns its firmness. Dryness and wetness are perceived when a dry object disperses the moisture in the skin’s pores, whereas a wet object increases it. Heaviness and lightness are sensed through the nerves and muscles.  

Taste occurs when an object’s moisture mixes with the tongue’s moisture, penetrating its pores and conveying one of the nine flavors we described. This is due to a membrane over the tongue’s flesh, connected to the brain.  

The olfactory faculty perceives when a fine vapor dissolves from bodies and blends with air, forming a subtle, spiritual mixture. If the vapor is pleasant, the air becomes pleasant; if unpleasant, the opposite occurs. When an animal inhales, this air enters the olfactory organ (resembling two nipples), and the faculty discerns its quality. Some animals, like dung beetles, thrive in filth but perish in flowers, while others, like scavengers, are repelled by perfume but attracted to decay—all due to their dominant humoral temperaments.  

Hearing perceives two kinds of sounds: animate and inanimate. The inanimate is either natural (e.g., stones, iron, wood, thunder, wind) or artificial (e.g., drums, horns, flutes). The animate is either non-rational (animal sounds) or rational (human speech), the latter being either meaningful (demonstrative) or meaningless (laughter, crying). Sound is produced by the collision or friction of two bodies, creating waves in the air that propagate according to their force. The auditory faculty perceives these waves, much like ripples in water when a stone is dropped, though air waves expand in six directions, unlike water’s surface waves. Sound diminishes over distance as its energy dissipates, akin to the weakening of a drawn bowstring.  

Vision differs from other senses in that it does not function in darkness. While the ear can perceive through barriers, the eye cannot. Thus, many philosophers rank hearing above sight, and the Quran mentions hearing first when listing these faculties. The eye perceives darkness but sees nothing within it; it perceives light directly and other objects through light. Colors manifest best against white (resembling light) and worst against black (resembling darkness). Light and darkness in the world are like the soul in the body: if bodies are pure, the soul acts as light does in transparent objects like crystal or glass. When light strikes a surface, its reflection carries the object’s color into the eye’s vitreous and choroid humors, which act as mirrors.  

These faculties in humans are like distinct limbs—each with its function, yet all governed by a single soul. When the soul acts in the ear, it is called "hearing"; in the eye, "sight"; in the liver, "the natural faculty"; in the brain, "the vital soul." The First Creation by God is called "substance"; when it governs bodies, it is "soul"; when it knows universal truths, "intellect"; when it governs plants, "vegetative soul"; when it governs humans, "human soul"; and when it acts in the liver, "natural soul." Thus, the soul of the entire universe is one, its light a single light—though its names vary by function, whether in the sensible or intelligible realm.
