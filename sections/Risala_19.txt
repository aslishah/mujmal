### Summary of the Fifth Epistle on Natural Sciences Concerning Minerals  
In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful  

Know that the generated and corruptible things beneath the sphere of the Moon are of five kinds:  
1. **Transmutation** of the elements—from fiery to airy, airy to watery, watery to earthly—and conversely, from earthly to liquid, liquid to airy, and airy to fiery.  
2. That which forms in the air, as we have discussed in *Meteorological Phenomena*.  
3. That which arises in the seas and mountains, such as minerals.  
4. Plants, trees, and all bodies that nourish.  
5. Animals.  

Everything beneath the lunar sphere, at the time of its generation, has four causes:  
1. The **efficient cause** (natural forces),  
2. The **material cause** (prime matter),  
3. The **formal cause** (celestial influence),  
4. The **final cause** (purpose).  

For minerals, the **efficient cause** is nature—the primal motion of the simple substance that permeates all bodies of the world. The **material cause** is mercury and sulfur. The **formal cause** is the celestial spheres and planetary motions, which impart potency to the four elements. The **final cause** is their utility.  

The differences in their natures, colors, scents, and forms arise from variations in soils, waters, and atmospheric conditions. The Earth, in its outward and inward aspects—with its caves, mountains, and plains—is like the layers of an onion. Each stratum has its own hue; some are hard, others soft. Every region yields substances suited to its climate, water, and soil.  

Minerals are of three kinds:  
1. Those formed in the depths of the sea, like pearls and coral (coral being plant-like, pearls animal-like).  
2. Those emerging within mountains and rocks over many years, such as gold, silver, copper, iron, tin, and lead.  
3. Those requiring vast cycles to form, like rubies, emeralds, and agates.  

The Earth is partly barren, partly watery, and partly cavernous. Over cycles, dry lands become seas, seas turn to land, and mountains rise from waters. Without this cycle, mountains would never form, for their origin lies in the sea. Over ages, seas become deserts, and deserts seas. When water settles in a plain, it inevitably stirs as the sea does—the cause of this agitation we shall explain elsewhere. Sand, soil, and stones are cast aside, and where the water once surged, it continues to rise annually, depositing new layers atop the old.  

Great floods bring boulders—weighing hundreds to thousands of *manns*—into the sea, where they settle and accumulate, eventually forming mountains and islands. When the water shifts over long cycles, the softened slopes and mountains allow it to flow elsewhere. Earthquakes then act with greater force than the daily churning of waters, splitting the land, creating valleys, rivers, and gorges. Where the earth is soft, cities and sweet springs emerge, reeds and grasses grow, and birds and beasts gather. Over time, people settle, build, and thrive—until cycles shift again, and the land succumbs to water. Such is the fate of earthly habitations, as decreed by celestial laws.  

We say that many minerals melt in fire and solidify upon cooling—gold, silver, copper, iron, tin, and lead. In China and India, other ores arise, differing from what we know, though rare and confined to those climes. Some resist fire due to hardness or lack of fusibility, like diamonds, rubies, and agates. Others are soft, melting into vapor, like salts, or persisting unchanged, like vitriols. Some, though not hard, elude fire—like talc and mercury, which flee heat, or sulfur and arsenic, which ignite due to their combustibility. Some must be calcined, like marcasite. Some are animal in origin, like pearls, or flow from springs, like amber and naphtha. I have heard that amber is a dew settling on the seashore, as are lac, ladanum, and mummy.  

The origin of fusible minerals lies in vapors trapped within the Earth, among stones, mountains, and caves. Solar heat cooks them into vapor, which, finding no escape, condenses back. In winter, heat retreats inward, overpowered by cold. By day, the air warms from the Sun’s reflection; by night, the icy sphere prevails, driving heat into the depths. The heat within caves and rocks, roused by its own dominance, rises but, finding no exit, settles again as cold returns. Each time it settles, it gains earthy density. Over time, it ascends and descends until it becomes mercury.  

If its nature is combustible—like the soil yielding naphtha and pitch—each condensation adds flammability, until it becomes sulfur or arsenic. Sulfur may be yellow or red, its hue and nature matching the soil. If sulfur and mercury lie apart, they remain inert. But if they meet in one place, ascending and descending together, they cling, and the mine’s heat gradually cooks them into a mineral substance.  

If the sulfur is pure, the mercury free of impurities, and the cooking balanced without cold’s intrusion, mercury and sulfur become gold. If pure but cold intervenes, silver results. If balanced but heat is excessive, gold burns into copper. If mercury dominates and cold prevails, tin forms. If mercury is abundant but sulfur scant, the soil sandy, and cold dominant, lead arises. If mercury’s parts are heavy, sulfur impure, and the mine swings between heat and cold, iron is born. If sulfur dominates and heat is extreme, antimony or marcasite results. Thus, all minerals derive from soil, sulfur, mercury, cooking, heat, and cold.  

Gems like rubies, crystal, and agate arise from waters and rains—fine vapors settling in high mountain caves, free of dust. Solar heat lifts these waters; reaching the peaks, they freeze, for lofty mountains lie in frigid zones, especially in the north. The waters condense, grow heavy, and resist solar ascent. Over time, they cook into gems, their hues reflecting the ruling planet or clime—crystal and diamond belong to the Moon and Jupiter, agate to Venus (with solar influence), ruby to the Sun and Mars. The Moon partakes in all, but is singular in jet.  

### On the Formation of Pearls  
Know that in the sea, oysters reproduce. Some say they arise from a viscous moisture gathered in the depths. When the oyster grows large and its interior swells, a creature forms within—a white flesh-like piece, akin to fish meat, of pure, delicate nature. The oyster moves by this creature’s power but never opens its mouth, lest the salty sea kill it. When fully mature, it rises to the surface at a fixed time, opens, and collects dew-like vapor from the sea. Once filled, it closes and sinks into soft mud, where its innate heat cooks the substance into mercury. Unable to bear the weight, the creature expels it, as a woman in labor. The oyster resurfaces, sometimes whole, sometimes fragmented, like spilled mercury. Over time, it hardens into a pearl.  

Among gems, gold, ruby, and pearl are most precious. Copper resembles gold, tin silver. I have heard that tin comes from arsenic and mercury, not sulfur—a plausible claim. Sages agree lead surpasses tin. Other minerals include marcasite, realgar, lapis lazuli, turquoise, amber, magnetite, and the like—much used by alchemists. This suffices as a foundation for knowing minerals, plants, gems, and more.  

And God knows best.
