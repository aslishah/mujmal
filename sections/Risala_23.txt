### Summary of the Eighth Epistle on Natural Philosophy Concerning the Composition of the Body  
In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  
Know that among animals, the one closest to the rank of the celestial is man, who is composed of a body and a soul. He is rational and discerning, capable of deriving universal principles, devising crafts, and claiming knowledge of things. Our purpose in this discourse is to show that it is fitting for a rational being to claim knowledge of the divine realm and the laws of religions—but only after knowing himself. One who makes such claims without self-knowledge is like a person who claims, "I feed others," while he himself is hungry; or one who says to another, "I clothe you," while he is naked; or one who treats the sick while he himself is ill and knows no remedy for his own condition. Such a state is deeply repugnant to the wise. Therefore, one must begin the pursuit of knowledge with oneself before turning to other things. Indeed, the early sages unanimously agree that whoever knows his own body and soul knows both worlds.  

Thus, understand that the name "man" applies to the composite of body and soul.  
-189-  
The body is like a house, and the soul is its master. In tangible terms, the body is like a shell and the soul like the kernel within; or the body is like the kernel and the soul like the oil; or, more precisely, the body is like the oil and the soul like the potency connected to it, which is bound to the body. In another analogy, the body is like a mount, and the soul its rider. When the two are mentioned together, they are called "the rider." Knowledge of this composite is attained in three ways:  
1. By examining the state of the body and what is connected to it.  
2. By examining the state of the soul and its unique attributes, independent of anything else.  
3. By examining the state of both together and what emerges from their union.  

As for examining the body, one must know and recognize every organ it possesses—skin, flesh, white and yellow tendons, bones, and internal organs—how many there are, the function of each, and what they do externally and internally. This is elaborated in the science of anatomy, but we shall briefly introduce it here.  

Know that man, as he is in himself, is a microcosm. The sages say that man is a "small world," meaning that everything existing in the celestial spheres, stars, natures, and beings—minerals, plants, and animals—exists within man, indeed in a more complete and refined form.  

First, know that man is not merely from the earthly elements (fire, air, water, and earth), which arise from heat, cold, moisture, and dryness—qualities derived from primal nature, the first motion we have mentioned. In man, yellow bile corresponds to fire, blood to air, phlegm to water, and black bile to earth. The humors of man are all derived from these four pillars, and the qualities of cold, moist, hot, and dry are the four powers within these four humors, which originate from the nature of the elements. These humors are metaphorically attributed to the elements.  

There are nine substances in man: bones, marrow, veins, tendons, blood, flesh,  
-190-  
skin, nails, and hair. And there are ten regions in the body: the head, neck, chest, upper abdomen, lower abdomen, pubis, buttocks, thighs, calves, and feet. The foundational structures, called "pillars," are the bones and joints.  

There are eleven storehouses in man: the brain, spinal cord, lungs, heart, liver, spleen, gallbladder, stomach, intestines, kidneys, and testicles. There are three conduits: pulsating and non-pulsating veins, the urinary tract, and the digestive tract. There are twelve (?) orifices: two in the eyes, two in the nose, two in the ears, two excretory passages, the mouth, and the navel.  

There are seven workers in man: the attractive, retentive, digestive, expulsive, growth-promoting, nourishing, and generative faculties. By consensus, everything in the higher and lower worlds exists in man. The layers of the body are like the celestial spheres; the principal organs are like the stars; the four temperaments are like the four humors. And as we have mentioned, all that exists on earth—plants and minerals—is also within man.  

It is incumbent upon the rational being to know himself in detail, not merely in approximation. To say "head" alone is like the knowledge a one-year-old child has of it. Rather, one must know it in detail: the body is composed of skin, bones, flesh, white and yellow tendons, principal organs (which are served), and secondary organs (some of which serve, while others neither serve nor are served).  

The head has many cavities. The foremost is the place of the brain, which is initially divided into three parts: one in the front of the head (with two chambers), one in the middle, and one in the rear. Within these cavities is the brain, the instrument of sensation and movement. The brain is enclosed between two membranes (called *ghishā* by physicians), the inner one being closer to the brain, attached like a gourd. From it, nerves branch out.  

This brain, situated within this gourd-like structure, is cold and moist. At creation, it was given this temperament because if it were hot and dry, it would burn, and if it burned, man would never possess sound judgment—any reasoning would be flawed. This is among the greatest graces of the Necessary Being, and we shall later provide proofs for divine providence.  

The eye is composed of seven layers and three humors:  
1. The white (*safīda*), called the conjunctiva (*maltaḥima*).  
2. The cornea (*qarniya*).  
3. The uvea (*ṭabaqa ‘unabiyya*).  
4. The arachnoid (*ṭabaqa ‘ankabūtiyya*).  
5. The retina (*shabakiyya*).  
6. The choroid (*mashīmiyya*).  
7. The sclera (*ṣulba*).  

As for the humors:  
1. The vitreous (*bayḍī*), between the arachnoid and uvea.  
2. The aqueous (*jilīdī*), which is the lens.  
3. The crystalline (*zujājī*), between the arachnoid and retina.  

All this exists so that if something unexpected confronts man—such as fire, water, a harmful beast, a hostile peer, or a deadly reptile—he may become aware of it. The benefits of the eye are numerous, as mentioned in medical texts, and this is among the greatest graces of the Necessary Being.  

Similarly, reflect on the ear: if it did not exist, how could one hear the voices of kin and kind? How could one learn or teach needed knowledge? How could one distinguish between ugly and beautiful, pleasant and unpleasant? If the ear took the place of the eye, in darkness where the eye is useless, the ear would serve as the eye. Where thunder strikes and the ear is overwhelmed, the eye perceives it—whether by trace or gesture.  

Likewise, consider the tongue, the interpreter of the intellect. Even if it does not fully convey what is in the mind, it conveys some of it. Without it, man would remain unknown, and the essence of the soul and intellect would not manifest from potentiality to actuality. What was hidden in Zayd’s heart would never become known to ‘Amr. Thus, the eye is the sentinel, the ear the guard, and the tongue the interpreter.  

Now, let one observe his own interior: from the crown of the head to the foot, there are 248 bones, each suited for a function—59 in the head, 24 vertebrae, 6 in the buttocks, 7 in the chest, 14 in the ribs, 4 in the shoulders, and similarly in the neck, 60 in both arms, 2 in the thighs, and 60 in both legs. There are 519 white and yellow tendons, each in its proper place—though detailing them would prolong this epistle.  

Likewise, the veins and arteries form a structure like a tent: the bones are its pillars and poles, the principal organs are its vessels and utensils, the rational soul is the master of the tent, and the natural faculties are like servants.  

Within man are three faculties, which no school denies:  
1. The natural (*ṭabī‘ī*), located in the liver.  
2. The vital (*ḥayawānī*), located in the heart.  
3. The psychic (*nafsānī*), located in the brain.  

The natural faculty has seven functions:  
1. Attraction (*jādhba*): When food enters the mouth and one wishes to swallow, the attractive faculty in the throat draws it down. Without it, food could not move from the stomach to the throat. Once digested, the liver draws it via this faculty.  
2. Retention (*māsika*): It holds food in the stomach until fully digested, preventing premature passage. Similarly, it controls the bladder and rectum.  
3. Digestion (*hāḍima*): Like fire, it cooks food in the stomach (the pot), with the liver as the cook.  
4. Transformation (*mughayyira*): Alters food’s substance in the stomach; the liver uses it to convert blood into flesh.  
5. Expulsion (*dāfi‘a*): Expels waste from the liver, stomach, bladder, and bowels.  
6. Generation (*mawlida*): Governs growth and reproduction.  
7. Nourishment (*ghādhīya*): Sustains all nutritive processes.  

The psychic faculties are three:  
1. Perception (*ḥissiyya*), divided into five senses.  
2. Deliberation (*mudabbira*), comprising imagination, thought, and memory.  
3. Motion (*muḥarrika*), centered in the heart.  

All these faculties are governed by a primal force—nature, which some call "soul" and others "intellect," depending on its manifestations.  

When the rational being reflects fairly, he will recognize this as divine providence. Why deny it? It is evident that when man sleeps, his limbs rest, yet the common sense remains vigilant. The lungs move the vital spirit, the heart keeps the arteries in motion, the liver acts as both cook and discriminator—sending black bile to the spleen, colorless blood to the lungs, and pure blood through the veins to the whole body, activating the seven faculties.  

The heart is like the sun, the liver like Jupiter, the lungs like the moon, the stomach and kidneys like Venus, the brain like Mercury, and the gallbladder like Mars. The body is a kingdom, and the soul its governor.  

Thus, through these proofs, it is clear that man is a microcosm: his bones are like mountains, his flesh like earth, his veins like seas and rivers, his brain and chest like celestial spheres, his principal organs like stars, and his psychic faculties like angels.  

This suffices on the matter.
