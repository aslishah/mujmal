### Summary of the Eleventh Epistle on the Categories from Logic  
In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  

Know that when the sages observed the existent beings of the celestial and terrestrial realms, the spiritual and the corporeal—whether lower or higher—they discerned through sound measures that all existent beings, apart from the Necessary Existent, are of two kinds: either "substance" (jawhar) or "accident" (ʿaraḍ). And "substance" is of two kinds: either "simple" (basīṭ) or "composite" (murakkab). Upon examining these divisions, they derived ten categories, which they named the "ten predicaments" (maqūlāt ʿashr).  

Initially, when they observed bodies and saw that they moved and grew—some by volition—and possessed intellect and sensation, they realized that a body, in itself, does not possess such power. Thus, they understood that there must be a substantial agent within the body from which these actions arise. They named this substance "spiritual," and they gathered all the attributes inherent in the substance and called it "body" (jism).  

At times, a body would become inert and cease to function—such as the death of an animal, the withering of a plant, or the transformation of water, air, and fire. From this, they understood that this substance is divisible: some of it is "simple," and some is "composite," yet both must necessarily be called "substance."  

-127-  

Thus, they called the "simple substance" the "genus of genera" (jins al-ajnās) and referred to the "higher" and "lower" realms as its species. Then, when they saw that bodies possessed dimensions—quantity, height, depth, width, and thickness—each with its own form and position, they gathered these attributes into a single genus and named it "quantity" (kammiyya), all of which are "accidents."  

Next, they observed other things that were neither substance nor quantity—such as blackness, whiteness, heat, softness, and moisture—and gathered these into a genus, naming it "quality" (kayfiyya), all of which are "accidents."  

Then, they saw names attributed to things—such as fatherhood, sonship, motherhood, brotherhood, marriage, and partnership—and gathered these into a genus, naming it "relation" (iḍāfa), all of which are "accidents."  

After that, they observed other names—such as above, below, left, right, behind, before, middle, this place, that position, and the like—and gathered these into a genus, calling it "where" (ayn).  

Similarly, they found names such as day, night, year, month, last year, the year before, this time, that time—and gathered these into a genus, naming it "when" (matā).  

Likewise, they identified several states—such as standing, sitting, lying down, reclining, and being cast down—and gathered these into a genus, calling it "position" (waḍʿ).  

They also found names such as "to him," "with him," "from him," "upon him," "near him," "for him," and the like—and gathered these into a genus, naming it "possession" (mulk).  

Moreover, they encountered names such as "he did," "he killed," "he stole," "he seized," and the like—and called this genus "acting" (yafʿal).  

Similarly, they found names such as "was broken," "was cut," "was released," and the like—and called this genus "being acted upon" (yanfaʿal).  

-128-  

These are the ten predicaments in full detail:  
1. Substance (jawhar)  
2. Quantity (kamm)  
3. Quality (kayf)  
4. Where (ayn)  
5. When (matā)  
6. Position (waḍʿ)  
7. Possession (mulk)  
8. Relation (iḍāfa)  
9. Acting (yafʿal)  
10. Being acted upon (yanfaʿal)  

Upon examining all existent things, they found that no statement fell outside these ten categories, and all were "accidents" except for "substance." This is an excellent indication, for this resembles the "units" (āḥād)—one of the ten principles, not a derivative, since all are composed from one, just as the Essence of Existence is the cause of all causes and the origin of all reasons.  

Among the properties of "substance" is that it does not reside in a place, yet it is the subject of things and can bear many predicates. It can be referred to, yet it has no opposite, admits neither increase nor decrease, is singular in number, accepts contrary attributes, and subsists by its own essence.  

Now we say that there are four methods of instruction:  
1. The first is "definition" (ḥadd), by which the reality of a thing is known. It consists of the "proximate genus" (jins aqrab) of the thing combined with its "essential differentia" (faṣl dhātī). For example, in defining "man," one might say: "Man is a rational animal," for among existent beings, there is no rational animal except man. Similarly, in defining "fire," one might say: "Fire is an elemental, luminous, blazing, and mobile body from among the four elements." Alternatively, one might say: "Fire is a luminous body whose natural motion burns things, separates the earthly, aqueous, and aerial components, and transforms them." One should not be constrained to express it in brief terms, for the truth cannot be known except through definition—whether lengthy or succinct—so long as it includes the "proximate genus" and the "essential differentia." If one mentions the "proximate genus," one must also mention all essential differentiae and attributes, near and far—as we did with fire, where "body" is more general than "element," yet it is correct. If one cannot find the "essential differentia," one may mention its specific property or proximate genus—as when one says of a horse: "It is a running, load-bearing animal that neighs." This is called "description" (rasm).  

2. The second method is "demonstration" (burhān), which we shall discuss later.  

3. The third is called "analysis" (taḥlīl), which is the unraveling of a "syllogism" (qiyās) into its parts. For instance, if someone employs a "fallacy" (mughālaṭa) and one wishes to understand it, they break down the syllogism into its "premises" (muqaddimāt), the premises into their "definitions" (ḥudūd), the definitions into "propositions" (qaḍāyā), and the propositions into "simple terms" (alfāẓ mufrada). Each is examined separately to determine its validity. If it is a fallacy, it is rejected; if it is sound, it is accepted and reconstructed.  

4. The fourth is called "division" (taqsīm). We shall apply division to the discussion of cause and effect to avoid prolixity, for there is no benefit in lengthy discourse. And God is the source of success.

### Summary of the Eleventh Epistle on the Categories from Logic  
In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  

Know that when the sages contemplated the existent beings of the celestial and terrestrial realms—whether spiritual or corporeal, higher or lower—they discerned through sound measures that all contingent beings fall into two categories: either "substance" (*jawhar*) or "accident" (*‘araḍ*). Substance itself is of two kinds: "simple" (*basīṭ*) or "composite" (*murakkab*). Upon examining these divisions, they derived ten categories, naming them the "Ten Predicaments" (*al-maqūlāt al-‘ashr*).  

Initially, observing bodies that moved, grew, and some even acted volitionally—possessing intellect and sensation—they recognized that corporeal forms inherently lack such capacities. Thus, they deduced the existence of an underlying substantial agent from which these actions arise, naming it "spiritual substance." The aggregate of attributes inhering in this substance they termed "body" (*jism*).  

At times, a body would become inert—as in the death of an animal, the withering of a plant, or the transmutation of water, air, and fire—leading them to conclude that substance is divisible: part "simple," part "composite," yet both necessarily classified as "substance."

Thus, they designated "simple substance" as the "Genus of Genera" (*jins al-ajnās*), with the "higher" and "lower" realms as its species. Observing that bodies possess measurable attributes—dimension, quantity, height, depth, width, and density, each with distinct form and position—they grouped these under the genus "quantity" (*kammiyya*), all being "accidents."  

Next, they noted other properties—such as blackness, whiteness, heat, softness, and moisture—which were neither substance nor quantity, and classified these as "quality" (*kayfiyya*), another genus of accidents.  

They then identified relational terms—fatherhood, sonship, motherhood, brotherhood, marriage, partnership—and grouped these under "relation" (*iḍāfa*), also an accident.  

Subsequently, they observed spatial designations—above, below, left, right, behind, before, middle, specific locations—and classified these as "where" (*ayn*).  

Similarly, temporal markers—day, night, year, month, past and future times—were gathered under "when" (*matā*).  

States of being—standing, sitting, lying, reclining, prostrate—formed the genus "position" (*waḍ‘*).  

Expressions like "to him," "with him," "from him," "upon him," "near him," "for him" were categorized as "possession" (*mulk*).  

Active verbs—"he acted," "he killed," "he stole," "he seized"—constituted the genus "acting" (*yaf‘al*).  

Passive constructions—"was broken," "was cut," "was freed"—were termed "being acted upon" (*yanfa‘al*).

These are the Ten Predicaments in full:  
1. Substance (*jawhar*)  
2. Quantity (*kamm*)  
3. Quality (*kayf*)  
4. Where (*ayn*)  
5. When (*matā*)  
6. Position (*waḍ‘*)  
7. Possession (*mulk*)  
8. Relation (*iḍāfa*)  
9. Acting (*yaf‘al*)  
10. Being acted upon (*yanfa‘al*)  

Upon examining existence, they found no proposition exceeding these ten, all being accidents except substance. This mirrors the "units" (*āḥād*), one of the ten primal principles—not a derivative, since all are composed from one, as the Necessary Being is the Cause of causes and Source of all reasons.  

Among substance’s properties: it does not occupy space yet underlies attributes; it bears predicates but has no contrary; it admits neither increase nor diminution; it is singular yet receives opposites; it subsists independently.  

Four methods of instruction exist:  
1. **Definition** (*ḥadd*): Reveals a thing’s essence via its "proximate genus" (*jins aqrab*) and "essential differentia" (*faṣl dhātī*). For example:  
   - "Man is a rational animal" (no other rational animal exists).  
   - "Fire is a luminous elemental body, blazing and mobile" or "a luminous body whose natural motion burns, separates, and transforms earthly, aqueous, and aerial components." Brevity is secondary to precision—the proximate genus and essential differentia are indispensable. If the latter is elusive, a specific property suffices (e.g., "a horse is a running, load-bearing animal that neighs"), termed "description" (*rasm*).  

2. **Demonstration** (*burhān*): To be discussed subsequently.  

3. **Analysis** (*taḥlīl*): Deconstructing syllogisms (*qiyās*) into premises (*muqaddimāt*), definitions (*ḥudūd*), propositions (*qaḍāyā*), and simple terms (*alfāẓ mufrada*) to verify validity—rejecting fallacies (*mughālaṭa*), accepting sound reasoning.  

4. **Division** (*taqsīm*): Applied here to cause and effect to avoid prolixity, which yields no benefit.  

And God is the source of success.
