### Summary of the Eleventh Epistle on the Categories of Logic  
*In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Know that when the philosophers examined the existent beings of the celestial and terrestrial realms—both spiritual and corporeal, lower and higher—through sound measures, they discerned that all existent beings, apart from the Necessary Existent, are of two kinds: either **substance** (*jawhar*) or **accident** (*‘araḍ*).  

Substance is further divided into two kinds: **simple** (*basīṭ*) or **composite** (*murakkab*). Upon thorough examination, they derived ten divisions, which they named the **Ten Categories** (*al-maqūlāt al-‘ashr*).  

Initially, when they observed bodies, they saw that they moved and grew, some by will, possessing intellect and sensation. They realized that a body, in itself, does not possess such power, so they concluded that there must be a substantial agent within the body from which these actions arise. They called this substance **spiritual** (*rūḥānī*), while they gathered all the attributes inherent in the substance and named them **body** (*jism*).  

At times, a body would become inert and cease functioning—such as the death of an animal, the withering of a plant, or the transformation of water, air, and fire. Thus, they understood that this substance is divisible: some of it is **simple**, and some is **composite**, yet both must necessarily be called **substance**.  

They termed the **simple substance** the **genus of genera** (*jins al-ajnās*) and called the **higher** and **lower worlds** its species. Then, when they observed that bodies possessed dimensions—measure, quantity, height, depth, width, and thickness—each with its own form and position, they gathered these attributes into a genus and named it **quantity** (*kammiyya*), all of which are **accidents**.  

Next, they observed other things that were neither substance nor quantity—such as blackness, whiteness, heat, softness, and moisture—and gathered these into a genus, naming it **quality** (*kayfiyya*), which is also an **accident**.  

Then, they noted relational terms—such as fatherhood, sonship, motherhood, brotherhood, marriage, and partnership—and classified them under the genus **relation** (*iḍāfa*), another **accident**.  

They also observed other terms—such as above, below, left, right, behind, before, middle, and specific locations—and grouped them under the genus **place** (*ayn*). Similarly, they identified temporal terms—such as day, night, year, month, past, future, and specific moments—and classified them under the genus **time** (*matā*).  

They further discerned states—such as standing, sitting, lying down, reclining, and being cast down—and grouped them under the genus **posture** (*waḍ‘*).  

Additionally, they noted possessive terms—such as "his," "with him," "from him," "upon him," "near him," and "for him"—and classified them under the genus **possession** (*milk*).  

They also identified active expressions—such as "he did," "he killed," "he stole," and "he seized"—and named their genus **action** (*yaf‘al*).  

Lastly, they recognized passive expressions—such as "was broken," "was cut," and "was freed"—and named their genus **passion** (*yanfa‘il*).  

Thus, the **Ten Categories** are fully enumerated as follows:  
1. Substance (*jawhar*)  
2. Quantity (*kamm*)  
3. Quality (*kayf*)  
4. Place (*ayn*)  
5. Time (*matā*)  
6. Posture (*waḍ‘*)  
7. Possession (*milk*)  
8. Relation (*iḍāfa*)  
9. Action (*yaf‘al*)  
10. Passion (*yanfa‘il*)  

Upon examining all existents, they found that none fell outside these ten divisions. All were **accidents** except **substance**, which stands apart—akin to the number one among the digits, being a fundamental principle rather than a derivative, just as the Essence of Existence is the cause of all causes and the origin of all reasons.  

Among the unique properties of **substance** is that it does not reside in a place, yet it serves as the substrate for other things. It can bear multiple predicates, be referred to, has no contrary, does not admit of increase or decrease, is numerically one, can receive opposing attributes, and subsists through itself.  

Now, we say that there are **four methods of instruction**:  
1. **Definition** (*ḥadd*), by which the reality of a thing is known through its **proximate genus** and **essential differentia**—as in defining a human as "a rational animal," since no other animal is rational, or defining fire as "a luminous elemental body that naturally moves to burn things, separating and transforming earthly, watery, and airy parts." A definition must be concise, for only through it can true reality be known. The **proximate genus** and **essential differentia** are indispensable. If one states the **remote genus**, all essential differentiae and attributes must be mentioned—as when we called fire an "element," though "body" is more remote but still correct. If the **essential differentia** is lacking, one may describe its specific trait or proximate genus—as in calling a horse "a running, neighing beast of burden," which is termed a **description** (*rasm*).  

2. **Demonstration** (*burhān*), which we shall discuss later.  

3. **Analysis** (*taḥlīl*), which involves breaking down a syllogism into its premises, then examining each premise through definition, proposition, and simple terms to verify its validity—rejecting fallacies and accepting sound reasoning.  

4. **Division** (*taqsīm*), which we shall apply to cause and effect to avoid prolixity, for lengthy discourse yields no benefit.  

*And God is the source of success.*
