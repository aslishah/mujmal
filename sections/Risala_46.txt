### | فصل ششم 
اما آنچه بعد از مرگ خواهد بود بر دو وجه است: اول آنكه شريعت انبيا بدان ناطق است، چيزى بر سبيل رمز و چيزى بر سبيل اشارت. و اشارت هم رمز باشد ولكن روشنتر باشد. و ما در رساله‌هاى حكمى گفته‌ايم كه دوزخ تن مردم است و زمين است، و مرگ تن زادن جان است. و همچنين گفتيم كه جاى هر جنسى از جواهر و اجسام كجاست، و جاى تن خاك است و عذاب وى آن است كه پيغمبران در كتابها ياد كرده‌اند. و اشارت ما درين فصل هم به تن است و هم به جان. اما عذاب جان آن باشد كه به عالم خاك بازماند و دربند هيولى بماند. مادام مشتاق عالم كبير مى‌باشد و آتش تشويش و حسرت او را مى‌سوزاند و هرچند هيچ آتش بر جوهر نفس كار نكند و نتواند سوختن. و اگر اينك جوهر نفس است تن بودى بسوختى يكبار و بازرستى. و اكنون كه جوهر نفس است هر روز هزار بار در حسرت آنكه چرا گناه كردم يا چرا نيكى نكردم مى‌سوزد، و آتش حسرت بتر از آتش اين جهان است.*** مثال آن چنان بود كه دو شخص را در يك خانه شركت بود و هر دو مايۀ تمام دارند و با يكديگر اتفاق كنند و گويند ما را از دنيا مايۀ نيك است و خورش و پوشش تمام داريم الاّ آنكه ما را دومعشوقه بايستى نيكوروى تا ما را بدان عيش بودى. و چنانكه خورش و پوشش هست لذت جماع نيز بودى. و اكنون ما را به سبب لذت جماع و عشق سفرى بايد كرد و از شهر نيكورويان

 بدانجا كه باشد معشوقه‌اى خريد. و هريكى جدايگانه به شهر خويش بازآمدن و هميشه به فراغ دل به عيش خويش مشغول شدن.- پس برين اتفاق هردو به شهر نيكوان شوند. و ازين هردو رفيق يكى به تماشا [ى] باغ شود بر عادت مردمان و در آن باغ جوقى بيند از ديوان و جوقى از خوكان و همچنين از بهيمه و حشرات چون سگ و خرس و مار و كژدم كه در يكديگر افتاده باشند و هريك طلب مراد خويش همى‌كند و بعضى يكديگر را همى‌خورند و هريك بلعجبى همى‌كند. پس اين مرددريشان عجب بماند و سبب آنكه تا ايشان با او خصومت نكنند آن مايه كه دارد به طعمۀ ايشان مى‌كند. و بدان سبب كه ايشان را مراعات مى‌كند بهرى آموخته مى‌شوند و گرد وى مى‌گردند و از بهر وى بازى مى‌كنند و اين مرد خويشتن را بدان مشغول مى‌دارد.- و اين رفيق ديگر هر روز بيايد و يار خويشتن را گويد اى برادر مكن كه اين مايه كه تو دارى نبادا كه درخورد ديوان و ددان دهى و ايشان با تو به شهر تو نيايند و از آن معشوقه كه شرط‍‌ كرديم فردا حسرت بايد خوردن.- و رفيق مغرور مى‌گويد خاموش كه اين جانوران كه تو مى‌بينى هر يك از بهر من كارهاى عظيم كنند و من ايشان را به شهر خويش برم و جمله معشوق من باشند.- پس آن رفيق ديگر از وى نااميد شود و از بيم گمراهى و مفلسى با وى مساعدت نتواند كردن. برود و معشوقه‌اى به‌دست آرد و به شهر خويش باز رود.- و اين رفيق ديگر با ديوان و ددان مى‌باشد و اين ديوان به زبان حال وى را مى‌گويند كه هرگز از تو برنگرديم و با تو به شهر تو آئيم، و برين حال مى‌باشد تا مايه هيچ بنماند. پس مرد اين ديوان و ددان را گويد مايه نماند مرا به شهر خويش باز بايد شدن. شما با من بيائيد. جمله با وى بيايند تا در باغ. چون آن مرد از باغ بيرون آيد در باغ ببندند و بازگردند و اين مرد تنها بماند. ناچار به شهر خويش باز بايد رفتن.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION ---

### Chapter Six  
Concerning what comes after death, it is of two aspects: First, that which the divine law of the prophets has declared—some by way of symbol, some by way of allusion. And allusion is also a form of symbol, though clearer. In our philosophical epistles, we have stated that Hell is the body of man and the earth itself, and that the death of the body is the birth of the soul. Likewise, we have explained the proper place of each class of substances and bodies: the place of the body is the earth, and its torment is that which the prophets have mentioned in their scriptures. Our allusion in this chapter pertains both to the body and the soul.  

As for the torment of the soul, it is to remain bound to the world of clay, imprisoned in matter. So long as it yearns for the greater world, the fire of anguish and regret burns it—though no earthly fire can affect the substance of the soul or consume it. For if the soul were corporeal, it would burn once and then be reduced to ashes. But since it is of spiritual substance, it burns a thousand times a day in regret, lamenting, "Why did I sin? Why did I not do good?" And the fire of regret is fiercer than the fires of this world.  

***  
An analogy: Two men shared a dwelling, both possessing ample provisions. They agreed, saying, "We have abundant sustenance and clothing in this world, save that we lack two fair beloveds to complete our joy. Though we have food and garments, the pleasure of union would make our bliss perfect. Thus, for the sake of love’s delight, we must journey from this city of beauties to a land where we may find such beloveds. Then, each shall return to his own city and devote himself to his pleasures with a carefree heart."  

Upon this agreement, both set out for the city of fair ones. One of the companions strayed to wander a garden, as men often do, and there he beheld a crowd of demons and swine, along with beasts and creeping things—dogs, bears, serpents, and scorpions—all entangled, each pursuing its own desire, some devouring one another, each lost in its own depravity. The man stood astonished, and to prevent their hostility, he fed them from his provisions. Because he indulged them, they grew tame, circling him and playing for his amusement—and thus he occupied himself with them.  

Meanwhile, his companion came daily, urging him: "O brother, do not squander your provisions on these demons and beasts, lest they follow you to your city! And as for the beloved we sought, tomorrow you shall taste regret!" But the deluded man replied, "Be silent! These creatures you see perform mighty deeds for me. I shall bring them to my city, and all shall be my beloveds!"  

At last, the other companion despaired of him and, fearing error and ruin, could no longer aid him. He departed, secured a beloved, and returned to his city.  

But the deluded man remained among the demons and beasts, who said to him in their way: "We shall never leave you! We will go with you to your city!" And so it continued until his provisions were exhausted. Then the man said to them, "I have nothing left—I must return to my city. Come with me." They followed him to the garden’s edge, but when he stepped out, they barred the gate behind him and turned back, leaving him alone. Helpless, he had no choice but to return to his city.  

When he arrived, he saw his companion with a beloved fair as a hundred thousand houris, surrounded by boundless provisions and all he desired. The wretched man now suffered a thousand hells at every moment he beheld their bliss. For all eternity, he must endure this sight, crying out in impotence, nakedness, hunger, and the torment of love: "Would that I had never been! Would that I were nothing!" Yet no relief comes, and he remains in endless regret.  

***  
Now, the two men are like Zayd and ‘Amr; their provisions are the days of life; their dwelling is the world itself; the city of fair ones is the realm of virtue; the beloved is knowledge and righteous deeds; the beasts and demons are wrath, lust, greed, envy, malice, avarice, ignorance, and sin.  

The garden is heedlessness and delusion; its gate is the grave; and its closing is the hour of death. Now consider: which torment is worse—this regret, or a hundred thousand hellfires? For those who reflect on the hereafter, this analogy is clear.
