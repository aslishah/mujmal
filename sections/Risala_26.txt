### Summary of the Thirteenth Epistle on Natural Sciences: Concerning Human Capacity for Knowledge  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

In this epistle, our purpose is to gather the sciences and demonstrate the extent of human capacity for knowledge—what can be comprehended according to human understanding and which sciences surpass human ability to grasp their true essence.  

First, we affirm what has been established in previous epistles: that man is composed of body and soul, the body belonging to the lower world and the soul to the higher world. We have also stated that the earth and all upon it are insignificant compared to the heavens and the cosmic order. Some may grow weary in studying this epistle, yet truth must be spoken, particularly in matters of proof.  

Thus, we say: When the wise observer reflects upon his own body, the five senses, the faculties shared among them, and the other powers residing in the heart, liver, and brain, he realizes that the eye, as one of the senses, has limitations. It cannot behold all things—when the sun rises at dawn, its brilliance overwhelms the eye, just as in scorching heat, the eye fails to perceive the sun due to the intensity of its light. Likewise, it cannot discern the minute particles of starlight, nor the full radiance of fire or lamps, nor the tiny mosquito, nor things exceedingly vast.  

Similarly, hearing cannot perceive extremely loud sounds, nor the faint footsteps of an ant—one due to excessive volume, the other due to excessive softness. Only what lies between these extremes can be discerned.  

As for taste, when one quality dominates—such as the heat of honey or the coldness of vinegar—the palate cannot distinguish between them due to that dominance. But when the heat dissipates and returns to moderation, both the sweetness of honey and the sourness of vinegar become perceptible.  

The sense of smell, too, when confronted with an overpowering fragrance or stench, fails to differentiate and is harmed by the excess.  

Likewise, the human intellect can grasp only intermediate levels of knowledge. If a scholar were to spend his entire life seeking to comprehend the abstract realities in the heavens or the celestial spheres, he would fail. The same applies to understanding the darkness on the moon’s surface, or knowing which peoples existed ten thousand years ago, what they did, or what will transpire fifty thousand years hence—such matters cannot be known through rational proof.  

However, what lies between these extremes—such as minor conjunctions occurring every twenty or thirty years (minor, intermediate, major, and greater)—can be known. But knowledge of grand conjunctions spanning seven thousand years or more is difficult to establish through demonstrative proof.  

Similarly, the Necessary Being (the Divine Essence), due to His majesty, is beyond the reach of intellect, and the intellect cannot encompass Him. Likewise, the "abstract intellect" (the form separate from matter) cannot be fully grasped as it truly is, even if one constructs rational proofs for it.  

What remains beyond our comprehension is like the creatures of the deep: just as we know nothing of their perfection, they know nothing of ours—though they may witness many wonders, what they have not seen is far greater. The same applies to spirits and the true nature of the celestial dominion—as they exist in their perfection, we cannot fully perceive them.  

Therefore, our path is to give thanks to God, the Exalted, for what we *can* apprehend through sensory and intellectual means, and for what we *cannot*, we must follow the guidance of the sent prophets, so that our worldly affairs may be ordered and our state in the hereafter made sound.  

Now, when we say that the intellect is incapable of nothing within its domain—that it reigns over all that is subject to it—this is absolute. But what lies *above* it, such as the Essence of the Almighty, if the intellect fails to grasp His true nature, this is not due to the intellect’s weakness but to the majesty of the Divine.  

What falls under the governance of intellect and soul—what man rightfully possesses, as established in philosophical texts—cannot be fully apprehended due to the veiling and obstruction of the body, not due to any deficiency in the intellect itself.  

What we do comprehend of intelligible realities is less than a fraction of what the intellect *could* know—for the intellect encompasses all that exists and all that will exist in the world. If there were doubt in this, the superiority of spirits over bodies would be nullified.  

What we *do* attain of intelligible truths comes only after great effort—ascending from sensory perceptions through disciplined practice, as if by a ladder. Evidence for this lies in the soul of the prophet and the soul of the seer: when asked, they speak of matters their bodies and senses do not consciously perceive. Likewise, when the seer or the ecstatic speaks, their senses are absent, for their intellect is turned partly toward governing the body and partly toward the celestial realm, aware of all universal realities. It conveys only what the listener can bear, translating it through speech.  

If the senses did not obstruct the intellect, the abstract intellect, the celestial dominion, the Necessary Being, and all we have mentioned would be perceived in an instant, as they truly are. Yet even now, the intellect *does* perceive—and if one person perceives, it does not mean others cannot. Many sages behold all that we have described, just as Zayd and ‘Amr see with their eyes.  

The opposite of this proposition is when Zayd claims knowledge of celestial realities, and ‘Amr denies it, saying, *"How can you know what I do not?"* Yet Zayd is not left helpless by ‘Amr’s denial, for ‘Amr cannot see what Zayd sees, nor can he be taught to recognize Zayd’s knowledge.  

Proof that the intellect knows all existent things lies in its nature: the intellect is a simple, uncomposed substance, like the celestial spheres and abstract entities. What is said to be beyond the intellect’s grasp is always *composite*, and these composites depend on the *simple*, not the other way around. Thus, the composite is incapable of knowing the simple, not the simple of knowing the composite.  

An analogy: Suppose Zayd builds a house and, out of kindness, constructs hidden chambers and adorns the walls with beautiful designs. If ‘Amr, having never entered the house but only heard descriptions, tells Zayd, *"You do not know what hidden chambers or beautiful designs exist in this house,"* he speaks falsely. How, then, can it be said that the intellect—which has constructed the universe and the edifice of existence—does *not* know? The truth is that one projects his own deficiency onto the intellect.  

If it is said that the soul, if wretched, does not ascend beyond the sphere of the moon, then—being abstract—nothing will be hidden from it when it perceives wonders. If it is blessed and ascends beyond the celestial spheres, the abstract realm and the celestial dominion will be fully unveiled to it. This is a necessary proof.  

Thus, when the soul, after parting from the body, perceives such realities—though it cannot know them now—this is due to the body’s obstruction, not the intellect’s incapacity. This is sound reasoning.  

Therefore, the intellect encompasses all existent things. This much is obligatory in response to this question.
