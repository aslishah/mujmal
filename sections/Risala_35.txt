### Summary of the Fourth Epistle on Psychical Matters Concerning the Intellect and the Intelligible  
In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  

Since it has been established that sensible things are all accidents, and bodies are forms in matter, and they can be apprehended through the senses—and since the senses are corporeal instruments, and whatever is perceived through a corporeal instrument is itself more spiritual—then the spiritual must be apprehended by something akin to it. That which is apprehended by the senses is the sensible, not the intelligible.  

We say that the first thing incumbent upon the rational being is to know his own body, for nothing is closer to the rational being than his own body. Thus, there are people whose senses are more refined and whose faculty of perception is more complete. We must first understand what perception is and how many kinds there are. In other epistles, we have stated that the senses are hearing, sight, smell, taste, and touch. But within us, there is another faculty of perception beyond these, and externally, there is imagination (khayāl), which is like a chamber located before the brain. Whatever the sense of sight perceives is immediately presented to it. If imagination remains in its proper place—for a reason we shall mention later—then when imagination reaches it, it acts upon it. If the faculty of thought (mufakkira) is in its proper place, it engages with it to contemplate its state, origin, and essence. If the purpose of this were merely the apparent state, the faculty of estimation (wahm) would be free from it. But if its purpose were the true meaning of that form, then estimation would act upon it—either grasping its meaning or failing to do so. Here lies a difficult matter for the possessor of knowledge, for estimation often presents things that the intellect, after much learning, knows to be impossible—yet they become firmly fixed in the imagination. Or if one denies them, they may call it disbelief.  

What estimation presents, the intellect may later attain, such as when estimation says that anything without front and back, left and right, above and below does not truly exist. At first, the intellect has no answer until it acquires the demonstration (burhān). Then it becomes capable of understanding the structure and nature of the universe, distinguishing between the world of bodies and the world of spirits, and knowing that anything with a head, hands, feet, face, and back must have front and back, right and left, above and below. Otherwise, if it lacks these attributes, it does not possess them. Thus, estimation departs, and demonstration takes its place.  

For example, suppose one forms a sphere, as is the habit of a globe, and places it before oneself, observing it without its instruments—hands, feet, head, face, or back—knowing that in the world of bodies, there exists something without these six directions. Then one may say that if it is possible for a body to lack these attributes, it is even more so for the soul. Thus, one realizes that as long as the intellect is unaware of logic, premises, and demonstration, estimation will occupy it with sophistry. But once logic is acquired, even if estimation presents something incorrect, the intellect will not be helpless; rather, it will examine its premises. If they are primary or sensible, it will acknowledge them without objection. If estimation has ruled upon them—whether true or false—it will submit them to memory, which is composed of parts of the brain.  

This faculty of memory is subtler than the faculty of the human soul, for if it were purely intelligible or sensible, it would retain all things. Humans possess another faculty of perception, which people interpret differently. Some say it is placed at the rear of the brain, while others say it is a faculty among the faculties of the celestial soul. This faculty is such that when the imaginative faculty (mutakhayyila) perceives something, it acts upon it, grasping the inner meaning of the thing. In the apparent state, non-rational animals share this with humans—just as a sparrow recognizes the form of a hawk even if it has never seen one, or a lamb recognizes the nature of a wolf even if it has never seen one. But in humans, this meaning is subtler, suited to human temperament. The philosophers call it the faculty of the soul (quwwat al-nafs), which foretells future events, while some call it "physiognomy" (firāsa), through which forms are interpreted. For example, they may say, "This form resembles such-and-such a beast; thus, it will act accordingly." Then they recall that action and call it physiognomy—but this is false, for it is merely likening one thing to another, as when they say a lion’s eye is red and full of arrogance, so when they see the same in a human’s eye, they say the man is courageous. This is mere analogy, whereas true physiognomy would foretell events before they occur. If such foretelling is consistent and without error, the person is called a "soothsayer" (kāhin), which is a lofty rank, for this meaning is above physiognomy.  

Thus, this faculty—though some claim it resides in the front of the brain—is connected to a royal, intelligible meaning, not a corporeal one. There are other faculties like this: the animal and vegetative faculties, but our discussion concerns this one.  

When the rational being reflects, other things become known to him that cannot be apprehended through these ten senses—such as the soul, its limit, its substance, estimation, the intellect, its attributes, the oneness and majesty of the Necessary Being (wājib al-wujūd). Thus, there are perceptions beyond the senses, which must reside in something else. This something cannot escape two possibilities: either it is a faculty within the human soul, or it is the human soul itself. Both possibilities are inseparable from the human soul.  

We have established elsewhere that the human soul is neither a body nor a composite; rather, it is a simple substance. But what we have said—that if imagination or estimation remains in its place—applies in moments of anger, fear, or desire. For example, if one sees the form of a beloved, even amidst many others, he is oblivious to all else. If an enemy of the lover or beloved is present, the lover, absorbed in the joy of gazing upon the beloved, does not see the enemy. Likewise, if someone is overcome by anger, even if many visible things are present, they are all distractions to him. The same applies to fear.  

Our purpose is to demonstrate that the knower, the known, and knowledge are one—just as the intellect, the intelligizer, and the intelligible are one. Thus, we must first understand what the intellect is, who the intelligizer is, and what the intelligible is.  

We say the intellect is a faculty within the human soul, for we have established elsewhere that the intellect is a luminous substance brought into existence by the Necessary Being before all else. It is the First Intention (al-qasd al-awwal) and pure goodness, and its act is the act of the Necessary Being, while other things are secondary acts of the Necessary Being. From the Necessary Being, the effusion (fayḍ) of mercy, generosity, knowledge, and all that exists in both worlds flows eternally through the essence of the intellect without interruption for even a blink of an eye. Whatever the intellect receives, it receives all at once—just as the air receives light from the sun or a room receives light from a lamp. Thus, the intellect bestows upon the soul, out of compassion and mercy, what it has received from the Necessary Being, and the soul accepts it all at once.  

The intellect has two aspects:  
1. Its luminous essence.  
2. The effusion of knowledge from the Necessary Being, which is also luminous.  

Thus, the intellect possesses two majesties and two faculties:  
1. The majesty and faculty of its own substance.  
2. The majesty and faculty from the effusion of the Necessary Being.  

Likewise, the soul has two majesties and two faculties:  
1. The majesty and faculty of its own substance.  
2. The majesty and faculty from the effusion of the intellect.  

What the intellect receives from the effusion of the Necessary Being, it gives to the soul, and the soul becomes knowing through the intellect. From its own substance and the effusion of knowledge it receives from the intellect, it bestows upon the entire universe. Thus, the subtler a thing in the universe, the better it receives. In the universe, nothing is nobler than the stars, then the celestial spheres, then humans. If there are other things with a refined nature or a balanced temperament—such as parrots and the like—they also receive some of this faculty.  

This substance and its effusion are most present in the stars, more in the fixed stars than the planets, more in the higher stars than the lower, more in the lower stars than the moon, more in the moon than in humans, and more in humans than in anything else on earth. Thus, what is attached to humans from the Universal Soul (nafs al-kull) is a substance from the substance of the soul and a faculty from the faculty of the intellect—which is the effusion of the intellect attached to the soul.  

An example from the sensible world is placing a crystal sphere on the ground, a crystal cup upon it, and drawing a circle of fire around it. The fire manifests its light in the cup, and the cup manifests its purity and the fire’s light in the crystal. Thus, the cup has two faculties: one from its own purity and one from the fire. The crystal sphere placed in the middle receives both.  

Here, the fire represents the intellect, the cup the Universal Soul, and the crystal sphere the human. The intellect is the light of both faculties the crystal possesses: one from the soul, the other from the intellect. The example of the fire and the soul is the inverse of the cup.  

Similarly, if sunlight shines upon a dome of glass and a person stands within it, the sun represents the intellect, the dome the Universal Soul, and the person the human. Thus, the human acquires two faculties: one from the sun through the dome and the other from the reflection of the glass. The reflection of the glass represents the soul, the sunlight the intellect—and this is quite clear.  

From these premises, it is known what the intellect and the soul are. But most people who claim excellence believe that the intelligizer is the human—that is, the human body—because it acts within it. This is an error, for in Persian, ‘aql (intellect) means "wisdom," and nafs (soul) means "spirit." Thus, it is the human soul that is the intelligizer by virtue of the light and effusion of the intellect, not the composite human. For wisdom resides in the spirit, not the body, and wisdom is the life of the spirit, just as the intellect is the life of the soul. The Necessary Being is the Living, the Sustainer of all.  

Thus, it is known what the intellect is and who the intelligizer is. As for the intelligible—know that the soul, by virtue of this intellectual faculty within it, is knowing in potentiality, not in actuality. The reason it is not knowing in actuality is that the soul has one face turned toward the body and another toward the divine realm (malakūt), the higher world, the Universal Soul, and the Active Intellect (al-‘aql al-fa‘‘āl). Due to its connection to the body and the density of matter, the knowledge within the human soul does not manifest. When it does manifest, it is in potentiality, not actuality.  

Thus, when one constructs a ladder from sensible things, deducing the unseen from the seen, and attains the intelligibles—what exists in the higher world, the soul, and the intellect—and distinguishes between the intellect and the sensible through demonstration, this intellect in potentiality becomes actualized.  

Let us give an example for clarity. Consider an unpolished steel mirror: in potentiality, it can reflect anything, but as long as it remains unpolished, it is still in potentiality. Once polished, with the aid of the sun, it reflects everything, and what was once potential becomes actual. Likewise, the human soul, due to its connection to the body, cannot receive knowledge from the Universal Soul and the Active Intellect because of the body’s density. But through discipline in mathematics, geometry, logic, astronomy, cosmology, physics, and related fields, it becomes polished like a mirror and receives from the Active Intellect and the Universal Soul just as a mirror receives forms. In this state, whatever distracts it becomes its veil.  

Thus, the intellect is this light of the intellect connected through the body, the intelligizer is the human soul, and the intelligible is the form of these things within the intellect. These three are one—just as knowledge, the knower, and the known are one.  

And God knows best.
