### **Summary of the Fourth Epistle on Natural Sciences Concerning Celestial Phenomena**  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Know that the cause of fire’s motion is the celestial sphere, and this fire lies beneath the sphere of the Moon, which is called *aether* (*aithir*). That which is closest to the lunar sphere is pure fire, but as it ascends further, its heat diminishes. Gradually, through several layers, part of the air becomes aether, with some regions warmer than others. When it passes beyond a certain quarter, cold overcomes it until it reaches extreme frigidity, continuing thus until it descends to the Earth.  

The reason the air adjacent to the Earth is pleasant is that vapors rise from the Earth due to the rays of the Sun and stars, tempering it. Were it not for the reflected rays of the Sun and other celestial bodies, the air surrounding the Earth would be overcome by extreme cold and frost. However, the reflection of solar and stellar rays moderates the air; yet if this reflection intensifies excessively, its power diminishes. Thus, the middle layer of air becomes extremely cold and is called *zamharīr* (intense frost), while the air closest to the Earth is termed *nasīm* (gentle breeze).  

Any phenomenon manifesting in the air—such as lightning, comets (*dhāt al-adhnāb*), and the like—arises from hot and dry vapors. Rain, snow, and hail, however, originate from warm and moist vapors. Rain, snow, and comets all occur within the sphere of *zamharīr*. Every vapor rising from the Earth must pass through the sphere of *nasīm*; some may linger in *zamharīr*, while others ascend to the *aether*.  

When the Sun’s rays strike a place directly—if it is a desert or dry land—a hot and dry vapor rises. But if the rays fall upon the sea or watery regions, the water evaporates, warming the air and causing it to ascend. The air above it then moves in five directions: upward, right, left, backward, and forward. This motion in the air is called *wind* (*rīḥ*), which is nothing but moving air.  

Similarly, when vapor rises and is carried upward by solar heat until it reaches the sphere of *zamharīr*, the intense cold prevents it from ascending further, making it heavy and causing it to descend rapidly. If the *nasīm* air is temperate, rain falls; if cold, snow forms; and if rain encounters a cold wind, hail results. If there are no high mountains to obstruct these vapors, they pass without precipitation, moving to another region where elevated terrain prevents their escape.  

If, due to cold, vapor rises after sunset or near dusk, the night’s chill prevents it from ascending, causing *dew* (*nam*) to settle upon plants and the ground. The formation of dew is akin to the condensation seen in alembics (*anbīq*) or steam baths.  

As for thunder (*raʿd*) and lightning (*barq*), their cause is one. Lightning reaches us faster because it is of a luminous nature, whereas thunder arrives later due to the undulation of air. The reason is this: when warm-moist and warm-dry vapors reach *zamharīr*, they recoil and intertwine. The warm-moist vapor cools, becomes heavy, and descends swiftly, while the warm-dry vapor encircles it. Wherever these vapors collide, a sound erupts, like hot iron plunged into water. The warm-dry vapor, trapped within, bursts forth in fragments, as when molten iron is drawn from a furnace. When cold strikes it, a spark leaps out, and when moist vapor meets another heat, a sound is produced.  

Whenever this warm vapor passes through moist vapor, it transforms into wind, swirling within the cloud and seeking escape. The moist vapor, burdened by its weight, ignites sporadically—like wind in a horn or the rumbling in the stomach—producing sound in all directions, resulting in *thunder*. Often, the cloud is rent asunder at once, and this is called *lightning* (*ṣāʿiqa*), resembling a bursting inflated bladder struck by a great stone, emitting a tremendous noise.  

Similarly, a thick, wind-filled cloud moves from below and above, its fragments grinding together like millstones. From this friction emerges *lightning*, just as fire sparks when leaves are rubbed together—a phenomenon well-known among the Arabs.  

The *halo* (*hāla*) encircling the Sun and Moon arises from moist air and rising vapor when cold is absent. The Moon’s rays reflect upon this moisture, casting their image in the air.  

The *rainbow* (*qaws quzaḥ*) forms from lingering moisture (*nasīm*) in warmth. At sunrise or sunset, its reflection appears, with hues closer to the Earth appearing darker or violet due to the density of terrestrial vapors. Higher regions appear redder because of the greater intensity of rays.  

*Shooting stars* (*shuhub*) and *comets* (*dhāt al-adhnāb*) stem from fine, dry smoke rising from mountains and plains, ascending to the *aether* where it ignites. If the air beneath the *aether* is still, it lingers briefly or at length before descending or moving through *zamharīr*. Most comets appear in the *aether*—a fine vapor or smoke so subtle that it traverses *zamharīr*, revolving with the celestial motion, ablaze until it extinguishes.  

These are the fundamental principles of celestial phenomena. *And God knows best what is correct.*
