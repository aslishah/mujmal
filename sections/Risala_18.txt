بسم اللّه الرحمن الرحيم
بدان‌كه سبب كون آتش حركت فلك است، و آن آتشى است كه زير فلك قمرست كه او را «اثير» خوانند. و آنچه به فلك قمر نزديكترست آتش صرف بود و چون فراتر مى‌آيد حرارت وى كمتر مى‌شود. هم برين تدريج تا چند ربعى از هوا اثير باشد و بعضى گرمتر از بعضى. پس چون از ربعى درگذرد سرما برو غلبه كند تا به غايت سرد شود، و همچنين تا به زمين آيد. و علت آنكه هوا كه به زمين متصل است خوش است آن است كه از زمين بواسطۀ شعاعات آفتاب و كواكب بخارات متصاعد مى‌شود و آن را معتدل مى‌گرداند. و اگر عكس شعاع آفتاب و كواكب ديگر نبودى غايت سرما و زمهرير در آن هوا بودى كه به زمين محيط‍‌ است. اما عكس شعاع شمس و كواكب مر هوا را اعتدال مى‌دارد و عكس چون بسيار تر شود قوّت او برود. پس آن هوا كه در ميان است به غايت سرد بود و آن را «زمهرير» خوانند، و آن هوا كه به زمين است محيط‍‌ است آن را «نسيم» خوانند.

### Summary of the Fourth Epistle on Natural Phenomena Concerning Celestial Effects  
In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  

Know that the cause of fire is the motion of the celestial sphere, and this fire lies beneath the sphere of the Moon, which is called "aether" (aithēr). That which is closer to the lunar sphere is pure fire, but as it ascends further, its heat diminishes. Gradually, through several layers, part of the air becomes aether, with some regions warmer than others. When it passes beyond these layers, cold overcomes it until it reaches extreme coldness, continuing thus until it descends to the Earth. The reason the air connected to the Earth is pleasant is that vapors rise from the Earth due to the rays of the Sun and the stars, moderating it. Were it not for the reflected rays of the Sun and other stars, extreme cold and frost would dominate the air surrounding the Earth. However, the reflection of solar and stellar rays maintains the air's equilibrium, though if the reflection becomes excessive, its strength dissipates. Thus, the air in the middle is extremely cold and is called "zamharīr" (piercing cold), while the air near the Earth is called "nasīm" (gentle breeze).  

-167-  

Every phenomenon that appears in the air—such as lightning, comets, and the like—arises from hot and dry vapors. Rain, snow, and hail come from warm and moist vapors, and rain, snow, and comets all occur within the sphere of zamharīr. Any vapor rising from the Earth must pass through the sphere of nasīm; some may remain in the sphere of zamharīr, while others may reach the sphere of aether. When the Sun’s rays strike a place at its zenith, if the land is arid, a hot and dry vapor rises. But if it passes over the sea or watery regions, the water evaporates, the air warms, and ascends. The air above it moves in five directions: upward, right, left, backward, and forward. This motion in the air is called "wind," and wind is nothing but moving air.  

Similarly, when vapor rises and ascends, the solar vapors reach the sphere of zamharīr. The cold of zamharīr prevents it from rising further, making it heavy and causing it to descend rapidly. When the vapor reaches the sphere of zamharīr, the Sun’s influence is cut off, and zamharīr repels it, forcing the vapors to turn back. If the nasīm air is moderate, rain falls; if cold, snow; and if rain falls while a cold wind blows, hail forms. If there are no high mountains and these vapors pass through, no rain falls there but moves to another land where high mountains prevent its escape. If, due to cold, the vapor rises after sunset or near dusk, the night’s chill prevents its ascent, and it condenses as "dew," settling on plants and the ground. This process can be likened to the condensation in an alembic or a bathhouse—  

-168-  

and similar phenomena.  

As for thunder and lightning, their cause is one, though lightning reaches us faster than thunder because lightning is spiritual (instantaneous), while thunder arrives later due to the undulation of the air. The reason is that when hot-moist and hot-dry vapors reach the sphere of zamharīr, they recoil and intertwine. The hot-moist vapor cools from zamharīr’s chill, becomes heavy, and swiftly descends, while the hot-dry vapor swirls around it. Wherever these vapors clash, a sound arises, like hot iron plunged into water. This hot-dry vapor fragments and bursts forth like molten iron drawn from a furnace. When cold strikes it, rays leap forth, and when moist vapor meets another heat, it produces sound. Whenever hot vapor passes through this moist vapor, it transforms into wind, swirling within the cloud and seeking escape. The moist vapor imparts its weight, burning sporadically—like wind in a horn or a gurgling stomach—producing sound in four directions, and "thunder" arises. Often, it splits the cloud at once and escapes—this is called "lightning" (ṣāʿiqa), akin to a wind-filled bladder struck by a great stone until it bursts with a mighty sound.  

Similarly, a thick, wind-filled cloud moves above and below, its fragments grinding together like a millstone, producing sound. From their friction comes "lightning," just as when leaves are rubbed together, fire emerges—a phenomenon well-known among the Arabs.  

As for the "halo" encircling the Sun and Moon, it arises from moist air and rising vapor when there is no cold. The Moon’s rays strike this moisture, and their reflection appears in the air. The "rainbow," however, comes from lingering moisture in warm nasīm, appearing at sunrise or sunset. The closer to the Earth or the darker it is, the more violet its hue, for the Earth’s vapor is dense. The higher the vapor, the redder it appears due to stronger rays.  

"Shooting stars" and "comets" arise from fine, dry smoke rising from mountains and plains, ascending to the aether where it ignites and moves due to the celestial sphere’s motion. If the air beneath the aether is still, it lingers briefly or long before descending or passing through zamharīr wherever its motion carries it. Most comets appear in the sphere of aether—fine vapors and smoke so subtle they do not remain beyond zamharīr but revolve with the celestial sphere, burning until they fade.  

These are the principles of celestial phenomena. And God knows best.
