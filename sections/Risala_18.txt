### **Summary of the Fifth Epistle on Natural Sciences Concerning Minerals**  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Know that all generated and corruptible things beneath the sphere of the Moon are of five kinds:  
1. **Transmutation** of the elements—from fiery to airy, airy to watery, watery to earthy—and conversely, from earthy to liquid, liquid to airy, and airy to fiery.  
2. That which is formed in the air, as we have discussed in *Meteorological Phenomena*.  
3. That which arises in the seas and mountains, such as minerals.  
4. Plants, trees, and all bodies that nourish.  
5. Animals.  

Everything beneath the lunar sphere has four causes at the time of its generation:  
1. The **efficient cause** (al-‘illa al-fā‘iliyya), which for mineral substances is Nature—the primal motion penetrating all bodies of the universe.  
2. The **material cause** (al-‘illa al-hayūlāniyya), which for minerals consists of mercury and sulfur.  
3. The **formal cause** (al-‘illa al-ṣūriyya), which is the celestial sphere’s rotation and planetary motions that impart potency to the four elements.  
4. The **final cause** (al-‘illa al-tāmma), which is the benefit derived from the thing.  

The differences in their natures, colors, odors, and forms arise from variations in soils, waters, and atmospheric conditions. The Earth, as it appears outwardly and inwardly—with its caves, mountains, and plains—is like the layers of an onion. Each stratum has its own hue and temperament: some hard, some soft. Every region yields substances suited to its climate, soil, and water.  

Minerals are of three types:  
1. Those formed in the depths of the sea, like pearls and coral (the latter being plant-like, the former animal-like).  
2. Those that emerge within mountains and rocks, taking many years to mature, such as gold, silver, copper, iron, tin, and lead.  
3. Those requiring entire cosmic cycles to form, like rubies, emeralds, and agates.  

The Earth is partly barren, partly watery, and partly cavernous. Over vast cycles, dry lands become seas, seas turn to plains, and mountains rise from waters—for without water, no mountain could form. The cause of mountains lies in the sea: when cosmic cycles pass, oceans become deserts, and deserts become oceans. When water settles in a plain, it inevitably boils as the sea once did (the cause of this boiling we shall explain elsewhere). Sand, soil, and stones are cast aside, and where the water once surged, it continues to erupt annually, depositing layers year after year. Floodwaters carry massive boulders into the sea, where they settle and accumulate until islands and mountains form.  

When the waters recede after long ages, the land softens, allowing rivers to carve valleys. Earthquakes then fracture the terrain, creating fissures, rivers, and gorges. Where the land is fertile, cities arise, springs flow, and reeds and plants flourish, attracting birds and beasts. People settle there, domesticating animals and building civilizations. But when cycles shift, these cities are abandoned, and waters reclaim them. Such is the eternal rhythm of earthly habitation—a truth evident in astrological laws.  

Among minerals, many are fusible by fire, solidifying upon cooling—such as gold, silver, copper, iron, tin, and lead. In China and India, other ores are found, differing from what we observe, though rare and confined to specific climes. Some resist fire due to hardness or lack of fusibility, like diamonds, rubies, and agates. Others are soft yet melt or vaporize, like salts; some endure, like vitriols. Still others, though not hard, elude fire—such as talc and mercury (which flees heat) or sulfur and arsenic (which ignite due to their combustibility). Some solidify, like marcasite; others are animal-derived, like pearls; and some emerge from springs, like ambergris and naphtha.  

The origin of minerals lies in vapors trapped within the Earth’s depths, compressed among rocks and mountains. Solar heat cooks these vapors, and when they find no escape, they condense. Winter drives heat inward, and nightly cold counteracts daytime warmth, forcing subterranean heat to rise. But when blocked, it settles again, accumulating earthy particles with each cycle—until it becomes mercury. If the substance retains combustibility (like naphtha-producing soil), it evolves into sulfur and arsenic. Sulfur may be yellow or red, depending on the soil’s nature.  

When sulfur and mercury coexist in a region, they interact over time, gradually maturing into minerals. Pure sulfur and refined mercury, under balanced heat and shielded from cold, yield gold. If cold intrudes during maturation, silver forms. Excessive heat turns gold to copper; mercury-dominant mixtures with cold produce tin; and impure mercury with fluctuating temperatures yields iron. Sulfur-dominant substances under extreme heat become antimony or marcasite. Similarly, rubies, crystals, and agates arise from rainwater vapors trapped in high mountain caves, free of dust. Solar heat lifts these waters; mountain cold condenses them, and over ages, they crystallize into gems, their hues determined by celestial influences.  

### **On the Formation of Pearls**  
Know that oysters in the sea reproduce or arise from viscous moisture collected on the seabed. As an oyster grows, a fleshy animal forms inside it, white as fish meat, with delicate purity. The oyster moves by this creature’s power but never opens fully, lest seawater kill it. At a fixed time, it rises to the surface, opens to absorb dew-like vapor, then sinks back into soft mud. The creature’s innate heat cooks this deposit until it becomes mercury-like. Unable to bear its weight, the oyster expels it—like childbirth—and the pearl hardens over time.  

Among gems, gold, rubies, and pearls are most precious. Brass resembles gold; tin, silver. Some sages say tin derives from arsenic and mercury, not sulfur, and that lead surpasses tin. Other minerals include marcasite, orpiment, lapis lazuli, turquoise, ambergris, magnetite, and more—substances alchemists employ. This suffices as a foundation for understanding minerals, plants, gems, and the like.  

*And God knows best what is correct.*
