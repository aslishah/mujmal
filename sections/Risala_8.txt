### Summary of the Eighth Epistle on Practical Arts  
In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  

Know that all existing things are either "intelligible" or "sensible," and apart from the Necessary Being, all are either "substances" or "accidents." Among them, some are "simple," such as the intellect and the soul, while others are "composite," such as the celestial spheres, the earth, and all that lies within them. Some are eternal, like the human soul and the soul of the celestial kingdom, while others are perishable, like those things subject to corruption after their formation—such as animals, plants, and minerals. In summary, anything whose form is separated from its matter perishes.  

Thus, we say that all crafts are of two kinds: theoretical and practical. First, let us define what knowledge itself is. Knowledge is the true form of a thing within the soul of the knower, as established by demonstration. Knowledge is attained in two ways: either through instruction or through reflection, and both are verified by demonstration. Therefore, whether through reflection or instruction, demonstration is indispensable.  

Alternatively, we may say knowledge is acquired through three means: first, through sense perception; second, through reflection; and third, through instruction. The validity of all three depends on demonstration. However, the focus of this epistle is on practice.  

Thus, we say that practice is a craft that arises from the ingenuity of the artisan, and it is a form subsisting in the artisan's soul. Every act has its "matter," and all crafted things fall into four categories: the human, the natural, the psychic, and the divine.  

The human category includes crafts like carpentry, construction, and agriculture—anything fashioned by human hands. The natural category comprises the forms of minerals, plants, and animals. The psychic category encompasses the arrangement of the four elements, the composition of the celestial spheres, and the forms of the world in their entirety. The divine category pertains to the primal matter and form, which emerged from nothingness without time, space, matter, form, or motion.  

An agent requires six things: matter, place, time, instruments, tools, and motion. For example, in carpentry, the "matter" is the wood, "place" and "time" are self-evident, the "instrument" is the carpenter's hand, the "tool" is his chisel, and "motion" is obvious. This pertains to human crafts.  

Some crafts may require five things, others four, or even fewer. Natural crafts depend on four things: matter, place, time, and motion. Psychic crafts require only two: matter and motion. The divine, however, requires nothing at all, for it is purely the creation and invention of the Necessary Being—exalted and sanctified.  

In this epistle, there is no knowledge requiring demonstration for the seeker except narration, and what is presented here and in other epistles. Thus, we have confined ourselves to this extent to avoid prolixity. And praise be to God in all things...
