### **Summary of the Eighth Epistle on Practical Arts**  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Know that all existing things are either *intelligible* or *sensible*, and apart from the Necessary Being (God), everything consists of *substances* and *accidents*. Some are *simple*, such as the Intellect and the Soul, while others are *composite*, such as the celestial spheres, the Earth, and all that lies within them. Some are *eternal*, like the human soul and the soul of the celestial realm (*malakūt*), while others are *perishable*, such as those things subject to decay after coming into being—like animals, plants, and minerals. In summary, anything whose form is separated from its matter perishes.  

Thus, we say that all crafts (*ṣinā‘āt*) are of two kinds: *theoretical* and *practical*. First, let us define what knowledge (*‘ilm*) itself is. Knowledge is the true form of a thing within the soul of the knower, as established by demonstration (*burhān*). Knowledge is attained in two ways: either through *instruction* or through *reflection*, and both are verified by demonstration. Whether acquired through reflection or instruction, demonstration is indispensable.  

Alternatively, we may say knowledge is obtained through three means:  
1. Through *sense perception*,  
2. Through *reflection*,  
3. Through *instruction*.  
The validity of all three depends on demonstration.  

However, the focus of this epistle is *practice* (*‘amal*). Thus, we say that practice is a craft arising from the execution of a skilled artisan, and it is a form subsisting within the artisan’s soul. Every act has its *matter* (*hayūlā*), and all crafted things fall into four categories:  
1. **Human** (e.g., carpentry, construction, farming—anything produced by human hands),  
2. **Natural** (e.g., the forms of minerals, plants, and animals),  
3. **Psychic** (e.g., the order of the four elements, the composition of the spheres, and the forms of the world in their entirety),  
4. **Divine** (e.g., the primordial matter and form brought into existence from nothingness—without time, space, matter, form, or motion).  

An agent (*‘āmil*) requires six things:  
1. *Matter* (e.g., wood for a carpenter),  
2. *Place*,  
3. *Time*,  
4. *Instruments* (e.g., the carpenter’s hands),  
5. *Tools* (e.g., the carpenter’s chisel),  
6. *Motion*.  
This pertains to *human crafts*.  

Some crafts may require only five, four, or fewer elements. *Natural crafts* depend on four: matter, place, time, and motion. *Psychic crafts* require two: matter and motion. As for the *Divine*, it is absolutely free from need, for all of this arises from the innovation and invention of the Necessary Being—Exalted and Sanctified is He.  

In this epistle, there is no theoretical knowledge that one seeking demonstration would require, except for narration (*ḥikāya*), and what is contained herein and in the other epistles. Therefore, we have confined ourselves to this brief exposition to avoid prolixity.  

*And praise be to God in all things...*

### **Key Philosophical Terms Retained:**  
- **Hayūlā (هيولى)**: Prime matter/substrate.  
- **Malakūt (ملكوت)**: The celestial/angelic realm.  
- **Burhān (برهان)**: Apodictic demonstration (Aristotelian sense).  
- **Ṣinā‘āt (صنايع)**: Crafts/arts (both practical and theoretical).
