Here is the English translation of the selected passage from the *Epistles of the Brethren of Purity*, maintaining its scholarly tone and philosophical nuances:

---

### **Summary of the Ninth Epistle on the Differences in Morals**  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Know that when God—Exalted and Glorified—willed to bring forth the children of Adam upon this earth and distinguish them with His wisdom and perfect power, He first created a being from clay, as the Qur’an declares. In ancient times, this being was called *Admanus*, as Idris (Enoch, peace be upon him) mentioned in his book. From his essence, He created a female counterpart, so that through them, procreation arose, and the world became inhabited. Their governance, the formation of their shapes and appearances, the differences in their temperaments, languages, and colors—all these He entrusted to the celestial sphere.  

From among these creatures, He sent forth prophets and raised up sages and philosophers to reveal divine law (*sharīʿa*) and wisdom (*ḥikma*). Divine law was established through revelation, and wisdom through rational proof. To the prophets, He sent scriptures and took covenant: *Whoever obeys Us, We shall grant eternal paradise—which is the heavens, the soul, and the intellect—and whoever disobeys Us, We shall destroy and cast into hellfire, which is the center of the earth.* As mentioned in the Book of the Israelites:  

*"O son of Adam, We created you by Our power for everlasting life—and I am the Living who never dies. Obey Me in what I have commanded you, and beware of what I have forbidden, so that I may make you such that you never die. O son of Adam, I am powerful such that when I say to a thing ‘Be,’ it is—without delay. Obey Me in what I have commanded, and avoid what I have forbidden, so that I may empower you to say to a thing ‘Be not,’ and it is not—without delay."*  

This statement is profoundly clear, and it is evident that the path of divine law is the best of all paths. The prophets and messengers did not command us in ways that would lead to our ruin—rather, they sought only our guidance.  

The purpose of this epistle is to expound upon human character (*akhlāq*) and how, when a person’s disposition is such that the world of spirits does not perceive it as tainted or blameworthy, they accept him. But if they find him corrupted and unworthy, they reject him. Do you not see how polished steel reflects the light of the sun, and from its reflection emerges an essence like fire? Yet if it is rusted, it cannot reflect the sun’s radiance—what use is a rusted mirror or a mere stone?  

Similarly, if a king or ruler has children, upon his death, his successor should be the one most adorned with virtues—not an idle, ignorant, and incompetent heir. Thus, we affirm with certainty that the power of the soul surpasses that of the body, for the body is subject to the soul, not the soul to the body.  

When a person habituates themselves to something through persistence, their nature accepts it, and it never leaves them as long as they live—whether it be moderation or gluttony, kindness or cruelty, indulgence or restraint, loquacity or silence, and the like. Whatever habit a person adopts, they can maintain. Thus, it is evident that morals (*akhlāq*) are acquired, and human nature is like wax. If a person is learned, their nature remains pliant like warm wax, conforming to their knowledge. But if they are ignorant, their nature is like cold wax, unyielding to command.  

Therefore, knowledge must be the servant of the intellect, and one must make reason the sovereign over nature. One must regulate eating, clothing, desire, and anger to maintain bodily equilibrium. For if one surrenders entirely to natural impulses, desire and anger will dominate, and control will be lost.  

That which exists in potential within the intellect—felicity (*saʿāda*)—cannot manifest in actuality without effort. The intellect is like a servant to desire, constantly scheming how to fulfill lust or act in anger. Thus, when the time comes for the soul to return (to its origin), it remains bound by these attachments.  

This state is like fire, oil, and wick. The wise know that fire is the most refined of the four elements, and each element is most suited to its own center. The nature of earth is to incline toward its center, and its suffering is when air, water, or fire divert it from its proper form. It cannot be said that any existing thing is devoid of pain or pleasure. The pain of earth is its ruin and corruption—for a tree, as long as it grows and flourishes, is in a state of being (*kawn*), but when uprooted and burned, it passes into corruption (*fasād*). By proof, it is known that existence is better than corruption.  

The comfort of earth lies in construction, cultivation, order in home and market, and the refinement of form. Its suffering is destruction. Water finds comfort in its center and suffering in its opposite. Fire’s comfort is in its center, for it is the most refined; descending into coarseness is its suffering. The refined naturally incline toward refinement, not coarseness.  

Thus, when fire is held in the realm of air by oil and wick, it suffers—for its refined form is imprisoned by its coarse matter. Similarly, anger and desire are to the human soul what the wick and oil are to fire. Just as fire is held back from its center by the wick and oil, the soul is held back from the celestial realm (*malakūt*) by desire and anger.  

Therefore, it is incumbent upon the wise to refine their morals and restrain desire and anger. Many other traits exist in humans, but all follow these two—such as greed, envy, and malice, which stem from desire, and hatred and vengeance, which stem from anger.  

When a person indulges in desire, gathering all means to satisfy it, they resemble a swine—with their intellect subjugated to bestiality. When they quarrel over worldly matters, they resemble a dog. When they seek sustenance through theft and deceit, they resemble a wolf. When they grow arrogant in these states, they resemble a lion. When wrath combines with pride and cunning, they resemble a bear. When hoarding wealth dominates them, they resemble an ant. When they harbor hatred unprovoked, they resemble a serpent. When they harm others out of weakness and fear, they resemble a scorpion. When they seek a mate, they resemble a rooster.  

If all these traits mix with wrath and become habitual, the person becomes a demon (*dīv*) in absolute terms—retaining nothing of the angelic, and the rational soul (*nafs nāṭiqa*) becomes imprisoned by the demonic.  

What people commonly call "inherited traits" from parents refers to this: the soul is the mother, and the intellect is the father. Thus, if the wise gradually refine themselves—first by reducing food until they are content with a hundred dirhams’ worth per day—their bestial desires will weaken, dignity will arise within them, their character will improve, and humility will become habitual. In time, they will necessarily abandon these blameworthy traits.  

In this state, they will be closer to the angels than to base humanity.  

We further assert that every bad trait in humans stems from desire and anger—and anger itself arises from desire. Though desire is blameworthy in philosophy, it is permitted in all religious laws. We cannot say that our Prophet (peace be upon him) was unaware of what we have mentioned—nor that he abstained from marriage. Nor can we say that Solomon (peace be upon him) was misguided, though we know he had three hundred and sixty chambers. Yet they subjugated desire to the rule of intellect, not indulging it out of whim but for the sake of wisdom and the order of the world.  

Let no one think that we praise desire or that any philosopher has ever permitted its excess—God forbid! The root of all corruption lies in attachment to desire, though anger is even more reprehensible.  

In this epistle, there are many admonitions, counsels, verses, and wisdom—but we have omitted detailed proofs for brevity.  

*And God is the Grantor of Success.*
