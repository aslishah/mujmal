اما آنچه بعد از مرگ خواهد بود بر دو وجه است: اول آنكه شريعت انبيا بدان ناطق است، چيزى بر سبيل رمز و چيزى بر سبيل اشارت. و اشارت هم رمز باشد ولكن روشنتر باشد. و ما در رساله‌هاى حكمى گفته‌ايم كه دوزخ تن مردم است و زمين است، و مرگ تن زادن جان است. و همچنين گفتيم كه جاى هر جنسى از جواهر و اجسام كجاست، و جاى تن خاك است و عذاب وى آن است كه پيغمبران در كتابها ياد كرده‌اند. و اشارت ما درين فصل هم به تن است و هم به جان. اما عذاب جان آن باشد كه به عالم خاك بازماند و دربند هيولى بماند. مادام مشتاق عالم كبير مى‌باشد و آتش تشويش و حسرت او را مى‌سوزاند و هرچند هيچ آتش بر جوهر نفس كار نكند و نتواند سوختن. و اگر اينك جوهر نفس است تن بودى بسوختى يكبار و بازرستى. و اكنون كه جوهر نفس است هر روز هزار بار در حسرت آنكه چرا گناه كردم يا چرا نيكى نكردم مى‌سوزد، و آتش حسرت بتر از آتش اين جهان است.*** مثال آن چنان بود كه دو شخص را در يك خانه شركت بود و هر دو مايۀ تمام دارند و با يكديگر اتفاق كنند و گويند ما را از دنيا مايۀ نيك است و خورش و پوشش تمام داريم الاّ آنكه ما را دومعشوقه بايستى نيكوروى تا ما را بدان عيش بودى. و چنانكه خورش و پوشش هست لذت جماع نيز بودى. و اكنون ما را به سبب لذت جماع و عشق سفرى بايد كرد و از شهر نيكورويان

 بدانجا كه باشد معشوقه‌اى خريد. و هريكى جدايگانه به شهر خويش بازآمدن و هميشه به فراغ دل به عيش خويش مشغول شدن.- پس برين اتفاق هردو به شهر نيكوان شوند. و ازين هردو رفيق يكى به تماشا [ى] باغ شود بر عادت مردمان و در آن باغ جوقى بيند از ديوان و جوقى از خوكان و همچنين از بهيمه و حشرات چون سگ و خرس و مار و كژدم كه در يكديگر افتاده باشند و هريك طلب مراد خويش همى‌كند و بعضى يكديگر را همى‌خورند و هريك بلعجبى همى‌كند. پس اين مرددريشان عجب بماند و سبب آنكه تا ايشان با او خصومت نكنند آن مايه كه دارد به طعمۀ ايشان مى‌كند. و بدان سبب كه ايشان را مراعات مى‌كند بهرى آموخته مى‌شوند و گرد وى مى‌گردند و از بهر وى بازى مى‌كنند و اين مرد خويشتن را بدان مشغول مى‌دارد.- و اين رفيق ديگر هر روز بيايد و يار خويشتن را گويد اى برادر مكن كه اين مايه كه تو دارى نبادا كه درخورد ديوان و ددان دهى و ايشان با تو به شهر تو نيايند و از آن معشوقه كه شرط‍‌ كرديم فردا حسرت بايد خوردن.- و رفيق مغرور مى‌گويد خاموش كه اين جانوران كه تو مى‌بينى هر يك از بهر من كارهاى عظيم كنند و من ايشان را به شهر خويش برم و جمله معشوق من باشند.- پس آن رفيق ديگر از وى نااميد شود و از بيم گمراهى و مفلسى با وى مساعدت نتواند كردن. برود و معشوقه‌اى به‌دست آرد و به شهر خويش باز رود.- و اين رفيق ديگر با ديوان و ددان مى‌باشد و اين ديوان به زبان حال وى را مى‌گويند كه هرگز از تو برنگرديم و با تو به شهر تو آئيم، و برين حال مى‌باشد تا مايه هيچ بنماند. پس مرد اين ديوان و ددان را گويد مايه نماند مرا به شهر خويش باز بايد شدن. شما با من بيائيد. جمله با وى بيايند تا در باغ. چون آن مرد از باغ بيرون آيد در باغ ببندند و بازگردند و اين مرد تنها بماند. ناچار به شهر خويش باز بايد رفتن.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION ---

### Chapter Six  

As for what comes after death, it is of two kinds: First, that which the laws of the prophets declare—some by way of symbol, some by allusion. And allusion is also symbolic, but clearer. In our philosophical epistles, we have stated that Hell is the body of man and the earth, and that the death of the body is the birth of the soul. We have also explained the proper place of each class of substances and bodies: the place of the body is the earth, and its torment is that which the prophets have mentioned in their books. Our allusion in this chapter pertains both to the body and the soul.  

As for the torment of the soul, it is to remain bound to the world of earth, imprisoned in matter. As long as it yearns for the greater world, the fire of anguish and regret burns it—though no fire can affect the substance of the soul or consume it. For if the soul were corporeal, it would burn once and then be reduced to ashes. But since it is of spiritual substance, it burns a thousand times a day in regret, lamenting, "Why did I sin? Why did I not do good?" And the fire of regret is worse than the fire of this world.  

***An illustration of this is as follows: Two men shared a dwelling, both possessing ample provisions. They agreed with one another, saying, "We have abundant sustenance and clothing in this world, except that we need two beautiful beloveds so that our lives may be complete. Just as we have food and garments, so too should we have the pleasure of union. Now, for the sake of this pleasure and love, we must journey from the city of beauties to a place where we may purchase a beloved. Then each of us shall return separately to his own city and forever indulge in pleasure with a carefree heart."  

Upon this agreement, both set out for the city of beauties. Along the way, one of the companions wandered into a garden, as is the habit of men. There, he beheld a crowd of demons and swine, as well as beasts and creeping things—dogs, bears, snakes, and scorpions—all entangled with one another, each pursuing its own desire, some devouring others, each acting in a manner most strange. The man stood amazed at this sight, and to prevent them from turning hostile, he began to offer them his provisions as fodder. Because he indulged them, they grew accustomed to him, circling around him and playing for his sake. Thus, the man occupied himself with them.  

Meanwhile, his companion would come daily and say, "O brother, do not do this! Do not squander the provisions you possess on demons and beasts, lest they follow you to your city. Tomorrow, you will regret failing to secure the beloved we agreed upon."  

But the deluded companion replied, "Be silent! These creatures you see perform great feats for me. I shall take them to my city, and they shall all become my beloveds."  

Disheartened, the other companion, fearing error and ruin, could no longer aid him. He departed, acquired a beloved, and returned to his city.  

But the other remained with the demons and beasts, who said to him in their wordless way, "We shall never leave you, and we shall go with you to your city." And so it continued until his provisions were exhausted. Then the man said to the demons and beasts, "I have no provisions left; I must return to my city. Come with me." They all followed him to the garden’s edge, but when he stepped out, they shut the gate behind him and turned back, leaving him alone. Helpless, he had no choice but to return to his city.  

-334-  

When he arrived, he saw his companion with a beloved fairer than a hundred thousand, surrounded by abundant provisions and all that he desired. Now, this wretched man, every moment he beheld the beauty of his companion and his beloved, suffered a thousand Hells. He was doomed to witness it for all eternity, and every hour, in his helplessness, nakedness, hunger, poverty, and the torment of love, he would cry, "Would that I had never been! Would that I were nothing!" Yet he found no relief from his suffering and remained in perpetual regret.***  

Now, the two men—Zayd and ‘Amr—are an allegory. Their provisions represent the substance of life, their dwelling the primordial world, the city of beauties the realm of virtue, and the fair beloved knowledge and righteous deeds. The beasts and demons symbolize wrath, lust, greed, envy, malice, spite, avarice, ignorance, and sin.  

The garden represents heedlessness and delusion, its gate the grave, and the shutting of the gate the moment of death. Now consider which is worse—this regret or a hundred thousand fires of Hell. For those who reflect upon it, this allegory illuminates the matter of the hereafter.
