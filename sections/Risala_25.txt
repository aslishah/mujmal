--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION ---

Here is the English translation of the selected passage from the *Epistles of the Brethren of Purity* (*Rasā’il Ikhwān al-Ṣafā*), maintaining its philosophical depth and scholarly tone:

---

### **Summary of the Twelfth Epistle on Natural Sciences Concerning the Growth of Partial Souls**  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Know that the condition of the human body and soul is like that of a fetus in the mother’s womb. If the fetus develops properly in the womb, with limbs such as hands and feet intact, and senses such as hearing, sight, smell, taste, and touch all sound and unimpaired, then upon emerging from the mother’s belly, it will attain all worldly blessings, pleasures, desires, and aspirations. Similarly, if the human soul is disciplined—ascending from sensory perceptions to primary intelligibles, and from primary intelligibles to higher intellectual truths—it will, through this discipline, comprehend the structure of the universe through geometry, grasp the celestial substances through the contemplation of intelligibles, and fashion from these celestial realities a ladder to ascend to the divine realm, thereby knowing the First Origin and Creator of all.  

When the foundation that governs it through political order is destroyed by some cause, this soul becomes worthy of that higher world and deserving of its place in the celestial spheres. And when it realizes that its body will not remain in the earth and that its soul will inevitably separate, then caring for the body and remaining attached to it would be nothing but ignorance. Once the soul is nurtured in the knowledge of the Origin and the Return and recognizes its eternal abode, it will feel aversion toward the body and yearn for resurrection.  

But if the fetus is impaired in the womb by some defect—such as muteness, blindness, paralysis, or the absence of hands or feet—then upon emerging into the world, it will attain none of its desires and will live in abasement, dependent on others for its appetites. It will gain nothing from its desires except longing and nothing from its aspirations except regret. The same applies to the souls of Zayd, ‘Amr, Khalid, and Ṣāliḥ. If they have not acquired the provisions for the Hereafter—knowledge, virtues, self-awareness, and understanding of the celestial spheres through demonstrative proof—and have instead spent their days in heedlessness, with beliefs contrary to wisdom and divine law, they will remain eternally in the lower world, which is Hell. The body will abandon them, and they will lack the power to ascend to the heavens. They will linger in the air and earth, and whenever they attempt to rise to the celestial realms, they will be unable to go beyond the atmosphere. At times, they will be in the icy depths of Hell; at others, they will be bound to earthly matter, enduring great torment.  

Just as the body’s nourishment is food—and if the body does not digest it, its functions will fail, leaving it unable to move, grasp, learn, or teach—so too is the soul’s nourishment knowledge. If the soul has not fed on knowledge, nurtured itself, or acquired provisions for the Hereafter, it will be barred from ascending to the heavens (the eternal Paradise) and from reaching the Universal Soul, the Universal Intellect, and the Necessary Being—the ultimate goal of all aspirations. It will attain nothing it desires.  

The likeness of one who has not gathered provisions for the Return and remains heedless of his ultimate fate is that of a people who journey from their homeland to a foreign city to seek wealth, intending to return home to indulge in comfort and pleasure. Some of them engage in trade, amassing silver; others occupy themselves with marveling at the city’s wonders—its adornments and the peculiarities of its inhabitants; while others idle away, saving nothing until their time grows long. Then the king of that city commands them: "Depart from my city so that others may enter and, like you, partake of its benefits and seize its treasures."  

All these people set out—some with provisions, some without; some mounted, some on foot—and they encounter a harsh wilderness, filled with stones and thorns, devoid of settlements, water, shade, or greenery. Those who are mounted and well-provisioned will travel with ease, reach their homeland, and indulge in pleasure, attaining their desires and living there all their lives. Those on foot but with provisions will struggle through the wilderness, consuming their supplies when strength wanes, until at last, after immense hardship, they reach their city and live according to their meager savings, gazing enviously at the wealthy who amassed fortunes through trade.  

But those without provisions—the idlers—will wander out of the city, imagining they can return home empty-handed. Soon, exhausted and weakened by hunger, the scorching sun, the darkness of night, and the cold, they will turn back to the king’s city. Upon their return, they will find their former places and shops occupied by merchants and agents. They will be left helpless, with no recourse but to labor for wages. If among these idlers there is one who has learned something from the agents or merchants—or has picked up a skill in speech, resorting to flattery, begging, or prayer—then he may fare slightly better. But those who have gained nothing at all will have to don the garb of porters or fieldhands, carrying the merchants’ goods from shop to home, barely subsisting until their clothes wear out and they are left naked. If by chance they obtain another garment from a porter, they will count it a blessing and give thanks, though they must still bear others’ burdens, endure humiliation, and suffer blows—for whoever hires a porter or laborer may pay them wages or strike them as he pleases.  

And if they cannot even secure a porter’s garb, necessity will drive them to sleep in bathhouse furnaces or to beg for scraps—or else to steal and rob, as is the way of idlers. If they are caught and killed in theft, they will be leveled with the dust.  

The transmitter of this book says: Let the reader judge fairly concerning the provisions for the Return, the nature of this world and the Hereafter, and the journey back to the realm of the divine Kingdom—for this parable suffices. I could devise no better analogy, especially on the spur of the moment. Whoever has read the *Book of the Summary of Wisdom* (*Kitāb Mujmal al-Ḥikma*) and then reads this Persian book will plainly see that most of what I have said is not found in the *Mujmal*, and there is no need to elaborate further on this account.  

Know that the city from which these people set out for trade is the realm of the divine Kingdom (*‘ālam al-malakūt*), and the city where they traded is the lower world (*‘ālam al-suflā*). The houses and shops represent the human body, and the inhabitants of that city are the animals, plants, and minerals. The king of the city is Nature (*ṭabī‘a*) and the elements, and what the merchants and agents have amassed are their deeds.  

The merchants symbolize the ignorant ascetic, the unlearned worshipper, and the scholar without works. The agents who execute the king’s orders represent the scholar who acts, while the idlers stand for the baker, the butcher, and the beast-driver—those who know nothing of the world beyond eating, sleeping, and copulation. The king’s command to depart so others may enter is like death and execution. The wilderness and the icy mountain symbolize the ether and the celestial spheres.  

The mounted travelers represent the virtuous agents and servants who have stored up good deeds, with their steed being knowledge actualized from potential. The foot travelers are like those who worship without knowledge, their provisions being supplication and their need directed to God. The idlers without provisions or mounts are like those devoid of knowledge and works, stranded in the cold and heat, the icy mountain, and the ethereal sphere—their return symbolizing the obstructed souls (*arwāḥ mu‘aṭṭala*) that cannot ascend to the higher world.  

The shops seized by others are like the human body and form, now inhabited by another recipient of forms, leaving the soul unable to engage with anything. The porter’s garb represents the pursuit of material attachment—to donkeys, oxen, or wild beasts—and its tearing signifies the death of the donkey or ox. Seeking another garment means attaching to the matter of wild or predatory beasts if the opportunity arises. Sleeping in the furnace denotes attachment to the matter of dogs or wolves, and begging resembles the state of a dog. Theft and banditry symbolize attachment to the matter of snakes, scorpions, wolves, or lions. Execution and hanging are like the fate of one who leaps from the city—his essence diminished, he inevitably meets death and becomes one with the earth.  

Those who learned something from the merchants or agents are like one who has done a little good but possesses neither knowledge nor works. Their clamoring on earth is like that of devils and corrupt, destructive spirits.  

This is but a brief allusion in summary form, for the states of the ignorant and wicked in the Return involve many discourses and meanings—yet this much suffices for an introduction.

### **Notes on Translation Choices:**  
1. **Philosophical Terms:**  
   - *‘Ālam al-malakūt* (عالم الملكوت) → "realm of the divine Kingdom" (rendering *malakūt* in its Neoplatonic sense as the intelligible world).  
   - *Arwāḥ mu‘aṭṭala* (ارواح معطلة) → "obstructed souls" (souls barred from ascent due to ignorance).  

2. **Stylistic Nuances:**  
   - The parable’s extended metaphor is preserved, with terms like "idlers" (*baṭṭālūn*) and "provisions" (*zād*) emphasized to maintain the ethical contrast.  
   - The transitive verb *parwarīdan* (پروردن) is rendered as "nurture" to convey the soul’s developmental journey.  

3. **Cultural References:**  
   - "Zayd, ‘Amr, Khalid, and Ṣāliḥ" are kept as archetypal names (like "John Doe" in English).  
   - "Porters" (*ḥammālūn*) and "bathhouse furnaces" (*gulkhana*) reflect medieval urban labor.  

This translation aims to balance fidelity to the original Arabic/Persian text with readability in English, preserving the Ikhwān al-Ṣafā’s fusion of philosophy, theology, and allegory.
