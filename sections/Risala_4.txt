### | خلاصۀ رسالۀ چهارم در موسيقى
بسم اللّه الرحمن الرحيم 
بداند كه موسيقى تأليفى است كه وضع و نهادن آن ارواح حيوانى راست نه اجسام را كه جسم را از آن نصيبى نيست. و موسيقى صناعتى است مركب از جسمانى و روحانى، و تأليف غناء و الحان از وى است. و هر صناعتى كه به‌دست مردم كرده مى‌شود هيولى و اشكال او جسمانى باشد الا صنعت موسيقى كه موضع آن جواهر روحانى است و آن نفوس مستمعان است. و تأثيرات او جمله روحانى است و نفس به سبب او حركت كند، بدان جهت كه موسيقار باشد. و اصل اين علم از سخنها [ى] مسجع نهادند تا به روزگارى كه شعر گفتن بسيار شد. پس وزنهاى شعر مقطع به اصل موسيقى كردند و بر آن غنا كردند و بناى تأليفهاى ضرب و نقرت و ايقاع بر آن نهادند. و تأثير او در نفس مردم ظاهر است مبيّن. چنانكه گويند كه در [در] دو قبيله دو مرد را خصومتى

### Summary of the Fourth Epistle on Music  
In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  

Know that music is a composition whose arrangement pertains to animal spirits, not bodies, for bodies have no share in it. Music is an art composed of both the corporeal and the spiritual, and from it arises the composition of song and melodies. Every art fashioned by human hands has a material and formal aspect, except for the art of music, whose essence is spiritual—residing in the souls of listeners. Its effects are entirely spiritual, moving the soul because it is the domain of the musician.  

The origin of this science lies in rhythmic speech, evolving until the age when poetry flourished. The meters of poetry were then adapted to the principles of music, and upon them, song was built. The foundations of rhythmic compositions—striking, plucking, and timing—were established upon it. Its influence on the human soul is evident and manifest. For instance, it is said that a great enmity arose between two men of two tribes due to a blood feud, and none could reconcile them. A learned man claimed he could reconcile them without any mediator. When he made this claim, he gathered members of both tribes and brought forth wine. Once the wine took effect, the man produced an instrument and played a melody crafted for this purpose. By the middle of the tune, no anger remained in anyone present, especially in the two men. They wept, embraced without a word, and made peace. The man who composed this melody gained great honor.  

It is also agreed among scholars that the master Abū Naṣr al-Fārābī—may God have mercy on him—once played a melody before the Caliph Maʾmūn, causing all present to laugh uncontrollably. Then he played another, and they wept. Then he played yet another, and they fell asleep, whereupon al-Fārābī rose and left unnoticed. This account is well-known in histories.  

We say that every people have their own kind of music—women have theirs, men have theirs. The lullabies of children, the lamentations of women, the wails of the Daylamites, the hand-clapping of the Iraqis, the chants of the camel-drivers—each has its own form. Some music suits war, some suits peace, and this is vast and evident. For instance, instruments are played in temples and prayer niches so that prayers are answered and sinners repent. In hospitals, they are played to heal the sick.  

The transcriber of this book has not mentioned here why music was established. We say: Know that when it was observed that sound and its arrangements have a profound effect on the soul—and the soul dominates over the body—this science and practice were established so that the soul might receive its influence, transmit it to the body, and alter the body’s state. Thus, if one is skilled in this science—knowing natural sciences well—one can cure any ailment without fail through a corresponding form of music.  

We say that music is song, and the musician is one who sings, either with the voice or an instrument. "Song" (ghināʾ) consists of arranged melodies (alḥān), and "melody" (laḥn) consists of successive notes (naghamāt), and "note" (naghma) consists of measured sounds (āwāz), and "sound" (āwāz) is the motion produced by striking bodies against one another in the air—this we will elaborate in the Epistle on the Senses and the Sensible.  

Know that sound is of two kinds: either animal or non-animal. Non-animal sound is either natural or artificial. Natural sounds include those of stone, iron, thunder, lightning, and bodies devoid of life. Artificial sounds include those of drums, horns, flutes, and silk strings, and the like.  

Animal sound is of two kinds: either articulate or inarticulate. Inarticulate sounds are the voices of non-speaking animals. Articulate sound is human speech, which is also of two kinds: either indicative of something or not indicative. Non-indicative sounds include laughter, weeping, shouts, and similar utterances. Indicative sound is speech that conveys meaning.  

Sound reaches the ears through the subtlety of air and the striking of two bodies. When two bodies move toward each other, the air between them is swiftly displaced, carrying the sound in waves to the ear, where the faculty of hearing perceives it according to its capacity—this occurs by divine power, the giver of which is God, as He says: *"He who has made for you hearing and sight and hearts—little thanks you give."* (Quran 16:78)  

These sounds vary in form. The harder and denser the body, the stronger the sound; the softer and smaller, the weaker. Thus, sound is divided into two categories: one called "striking" (kūftan), which is the impact of two bodies, like stone, hand, or objects colliding—such as nails, tambourines, or the plucking of a lute—and the like. The second is "blowing" (sapūkhtan), the expulsion of air through a hollow object, like the lungs of animals, flutes, reeds, trumpets, and the like.  

Some animals lack lungs yet produce sound, such as bees, flies, and mosquitoes—their sound comes from their wings. Fish, crabs, and most aquatic creatures lack sound because they have neither lungs nor wings. For sound in animals arises from the lungs by the striking and expulsion of air through the lung's passages by the Creator’s grace and the vitality of the animal spirit.  

Melodies and composed notes arise only from the succession of sounds and their repeated motions. There is no motion in the sublunary world without a subsequent rest. Just as sound depends on succession, so does rest.  

Motion is either local—movement from one place to another in time—and its opposite is rest, for when motion ceases, rest necessarily follows. Both exist in time. Motion may be swift or slow—termed "rapid" (sarīʿ) and "slow" (baṭīʾ). Rapid motion covers a long distance in little time; slow motion covers a short distance in much time. If both are equal, it is neither slow nor rapid. If time is less than motion, it is called "rapid"; if time exceeds motion, it is called "slow." Rest is the cessation of a moving thing.  

We say that sounds are divided into eight types in terms of quantity, each pair opposites:  
1. Great (ʿaẓīm) and small (ṣaghīr)  
2. Rapid (sarīʿ) and slow (baṭīʾ)  
3. Sharp (ḥādd) and thick (ghalīẓ)  
4. Loud (jahīr) and faint (khafīf)  

In Persian, these are: large and small, light and heavy, sharp and coarse, open and hidden—though "hidden" may also be called "light."  

"Great" is like a kettledrum; "small" like a small drum. If you compare the kettledrum to thunder, thunder is great and the drum small. Lightning is greater than thunder, and so on.  

"Rapid" and "slow" are like the blacksmith’s hammer blows and the rower’s strokes.  

"Sharp" and "thick" are like high, middle, and low pitches. In another sense, "thick" is absolute, and "sharp" is constrained.  

The ranks of "loud" and "faint" are like "absolute" and "metaphorical," which we will revisit later.  

From another perspective, there are two kinds: "continuous" (muttaṣil) and "discrete" (munfaṣil). "Discrete" is like the beats of a tambourine, drum, or plucking of a lute. "Continuous" is like the sound of a flute.  

Continuous sound is either sharp or thick. One must observe the instrument: the narrower its cavity, the sharper the sound; the wider, the thicker. The closer the hole is to the blowing point, the sharper the sound; the farther, the thicker.  

If silk strings are of equal thickness and tension, their sound will be uniform. The finer and softer the string, the sharper its sound—this is all a matter of variation.  

When composition follows proper proportion and the instrument is well-tuned, it delights the soul. If it lacks proportion and is discordant, nature recoils from it.  

Sharp sound is hot and dry; it warms the temperament and thicks dense humors. Thick sound is cold and moist; it cools the temperament, especially if it is hot and dry. Balanced sound—between sharp and thick—maintains all temperaments in their proper state.  

Sound that deviates from balance disturbs all humors if excessive, like thunder, which may bring sudden death. Harmonious, measured, and proportionate sounds balance the temperament, stir nature, and may induce ecstasy.  

For every bodily and spiritual ailment, there is a corresponding form of music that restores health—just as for every health, there is a music that may bring illness.  

The foundation of musical science is mathematics. Just as number is infinite, and sound is infinite, so too are melodies infinite.  

We say that "song" (ghināʾ) is composed of melodies (alḥān), "melodies" are composed of notes (naghamāt), and "notes" are composed of rhythm (īqāʿ) and plucking (naqra). The root of all is motion and rest.  

Metrical speech and poetry are analogous: poetry is composed of hemistichs, hemistichs of metrical feet (mafāʿīl), and metrical feet of "causes" (sabab), "pegs" (watad), and "intervals" (fāṣila). The foundation of all is moving and stationary letters, as elaborated in prosody. When combined, they form nouns, verbs, and particles.  

In musical science, prosody is needed to distinguish "irregular" (zaḥāf) from "regular" (mustawī), just as "metaphorical" (majāz) is distinguished from "literal" (ḥaqīqa).  

There are eight metrical segments:  
1. Faʿūlun  
2. Fāʿilun  
3. Mafāʿilun  
4. Mafāʿīlun  
5. Mutaqāʿilun  
6. Mustafʿilun  
7. Fāʿilātun  
8. Mafʿūlātun  

Each of these eight is composed of three principles: "cause" (sabab), "peg" (watad), and "interval" (fāṣila).  

A "cause" is two letters: one moving, one stationary, like "hal" and "man"—in Persian, "sar" (head) and "dil" (heart).  

A "peg" is three letters: two moving, one stationary, like "naʿam," "ʿalā," and "balā"—in Persian, "saram" (my head) and "dilam" (my heart).  

An "interval" is four letters: three moving, one stationary, like "ʿalamtu" and "faʿaltu"—in Persian, "baravam" (I go) and "bokonam" (I do).  

Thus, the universal law of song and melodies is likewise based on three principles: "cause," "peg," and "interval." All notes are composed of these principles, just as in prosody, "cause," "peg," and "interval" are combined.  

In melodies, the same is done—though not as strictly as in prosody, where three or four "causes" cannot be strung together. In melodies, "pegs" and "causes" may mix freely.  

The origin of all is motion, which is like a point or the number one. The "cause" (two letters) is like intellect, line, or the number two. The "peg" (three letters) is like soul, surface, or the number three. The "interval" (four letters) is like body, matter, or the number four.  

Thus, motion is the origin of all, just as the Necessary Being is the origin of all existence.  

Our purpose in this epistle is not to teach the science of song and melodies but to demonstrate that in every science and art, there is independent proof for the existence of the Necessary Being—denial of which is impossible.  

Just as there is no number without one, no line without a point, no song without motion—and as one is found in every part of arithmetic, a point in every figure, and motion in every part of music—so too in every individual existent, the Necessary Being is found, and no existent can be without Him.  

Yet the nature of the Necessary Being cannot be pondered, for we cannot even conceive the nature of our own souls—how then can we know His existence as it truly is?  

From what we have mentioned, proof is evident, as we will elaborate in logical discourses.  

We say the instruments of this art are many: the harp (chang), lute (rabāb), barbat (ʿūd), flute (nāy), reed (bīsheh), tanbur (ṭanbūr), trumpet (surnāy), and organ (urghunūn). Many others have been devised, but none are as perfect and complete as the barbat, for all others have flaws and discordant proportions—except the barbat.  

There is a kind called "Transoxanian" (mā warāʾ al-nahrī), which is complete but not perfect. The barbat is perfect and complete, with no discord in its proportions—provided its form adheres to proper ratios.  

We have composed a book on these ratios, particularly on composite ratios, temporal motions, plucking, and rhythms—all elaborated therein.  

To properly construct this instrument, first shape its body so that its length is one and a half times its width. The belly’s depth—from back to front—should be half its width. The neck should be one-fourth the length of the body. The face should be of hard, thin, and light wood, and other parts as customary.  

Then stretch four silk strings, arranged according to the "interval" ratio. These four are named:  
1. "High" (zīr)  
2. "Second" (mathnā)  
3. "Third" (muthallath)  
4. "Bass" (bamm)  

The thickness of the bass string should be like the third string plus a third more. The third string should be like the second string plus a third more. The second string should be like the high string plus a third more.  

Thus, the bass string is of "bīd" silk, the third of "muḥ" silk, the second of "lū" silk, and the high of "kāz" silk—twisted and lightly moistened.  

Then tune each string to its proper place. Divide the high string into four parts from the midpoint and tie the first fret (dastān) at the fourth division. From this fret to the neck’s end, tie six frets, as detailed in our book on composite ratios.  

Thus, seven frets are obtained, representing the truth of eight—for there are seven frets and one open string (jūdāna).  

Tune the high string to the desired pitch and adjust until correct. The "second" (mathnā) string should be tuned slightly lower so that when the finger presses the seventh fret, its pitch matches the open high string.  

The "third" (muthallath) string is tuned similarly to the second at the seventh fret, and the bass string to the third in the same manner.  

These four strings form a circle, for "high," "second," "third," and "bass" blend and cycle. All infinite melodies can be derived from these seven frets and the open string, for each string has four pitches: open, index, middle, and little finger.  

These four strings and their arrangement are like the four principles mentioned in the Epistle on Number, from which all arithmetic can be derived ad infinitum. Likewise, from these four strings, all modes and melodies can be composed without end.  

Know that the high string corresponds to the nature of yellow bile (ṣafrā), the second to blood (dam), the third to phlegm (balgham), and the bass to black bile (sawdā).  

Thus, if blood dominates, its opposite—black bile—must be countered, so the bass should be emphasized. If phlegm dominates, the high string should be played. If yellow bile dominates, the third string should be played—for it corresponds to phlegm (cold and moist), countering yellow bile (hot and dry). The high string corresponds to yellow bile and exacerbates its heat and dryness. The opposite of yellow bile is cold and moist.  

If black bile dominates, the second string should be played.  

The high string corresponds to fire (hot and dry), the second to air (hot and moist), the third to water (cold and moist), and the bass to earth (cold and dry).  

Some say the third corresponds to earth and the bass to water—but if a high string is played with the bass, it becomes cold and dry. Thus, the third corresponds to water, the bass to earth—this is the natural order.  

One who masters this can cure all ailments without medicine, especially those of the soul.  

The transcriber notes that in this collection, it is claimed that the celestial spheres and stars produce sounds akin to instruments but more refined and pleasurable. Aristotle, Plato, Ptolemy, and their followers deny this, calling it impossible.  

The truth is that if the spheres and stars produce sound, it is spiritual, not corporeal. We have said that we will avoid fables in this book and speak only of proof.  

Ptolemy says that the celestial sphere is the largest body—if it produced sound, it would nullify all other sounds, which is unnecessary. The sound of the sphere is likened to thunder, lightning, and earthquakes, but the sphere cannot share the same attributes as natural bodies.  

Alternatively, their sound may exist but dissipate due to distance. Or their sound may be so subtle that its waves travel far.  

Another view: nothing on earth lacks a counterpart in heaven. Thus, if such refined music exists on earth, why not in heaven—even if spiritual and of a celestial archetype?  

It is also agreed that the stars are living, rational, and act by will—though their actions may differ, their life, speech, discernment, and knowledge of the first and last things are undisputed. Thus, they may produce harmonious, pleasant sounds—though one cannot definitively say they do not.
