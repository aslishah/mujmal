### **Summary of the First Epistle on Natural Sciences: On Matter (Hyle) and Form**  
*(Also known as "Samʿ al-Kiyān")*  

**In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful**  

Know that the great philosophers have established certain terms such that if others attempt to replace them with different words or offer explanations that do not suit their meanings, it would be improper. Examples include terms like *jawhar* (substance), *ʿaraḍ* (accident), *hayūlā* (matter), *usṭuqus* (element), *ummahāt* (primary principles), *arkān* (foundations), *anāṣir* (elements), and *ākhshīj* (constituents). If one substitutes these terms with others, it would either be displeasing or incorrect.  

The meaning of the philosophers' statement regarding *hayūlā* (matter) is that it is a substance receptive to form (*ṣūrah*). The shape that emerges within a substance is called *form*, and the diversity of existents arises primarily from form. For instance, we observe many things whose matter is one but whose forms differ—such as iron, from which swords, knives, saws, axes, and chisels are made. The matter is one, but the forms vary. Similarly, wood serves as the matter for beds, thrones, and other objects.  

**Matter is of four kinds:**  
1. **Artificial Matter (*Hayūlā Ṣināʿiyyah*):** The material craftsmen use to shape forms, such as wood for carpenters, iron for blacksmiths, clay and water for builders, and thread for weavers.  
2. **Natural Matter (*Hayūlā Ṭabīʿiyyah*):** Such as fire, air, water, and earth—everything that appears from beneath the lunar sphere to the center of the earth, including minerals, plants, and animals. When these things decay and their foundational elements dissolve, their forces return to their origins: watery to water, airy to air, fiery to fire, and earthy to earth. That which performs this action is *nature*—a simple substantial force originating from the first motion, as previously mentioned.  
3. **Universal Matter (*Hayūlā Kullī*):** This is absolute body (*jism muṭlaq*), from which the order and structure of the entire cosmos arises, including the celestial spheres, stars, and their contents.  
4. **Prime Matter (*Hayūlā Ūlā*):** This is a simple, intelligible substance beyond sensory perception. It is the form of existence itself—absolute being, devoid of quantity or quality. It is the first creation of the Maker, the First Principle, and the origin of all things.  

Know that existence, quantity, and quality are simple forms that only the intellect can grasp. When they combine, a secondary existence emerges: some become matter, others form. *Quality* resembles form, while *quantity* serves as its matter.  

**An example from the sensory world:**  
- A shirt is the form of a garment, and the garment is its matter.  
- The garment is the form of thread, and thread is its matter.  
- Thread is the form of cotton, and cotton is its matter.  
- Cotton is the form of the plant, and the plant is its matter.  
- The plant is the form of the elements, and the elements are its matter.  
- The elements are the form of body, and body is their matter.  
- Body is the form of substance, and substance is its matter.  
- Simple substance is the supreme form of all, nobler than everything else.

### **Chapter: On Place**  

Know that, according to most philosophers, *place* is the existence in which another existence resides. Alternatively, it is said that place is the encompassing surface of a thing. The truth-seekers hold that place is the measure of a body. Various opinions exist regarding place: some say it is a *body*, arguing that the place of a body must itself be a body, not an accident—this is evident in sensory experience. Place in a simple substance is like Plato’s soul in its habitual abode.  

Most materialists deny the partial soul, unaware that if the soul were not distinct in meaning (if not in form), all creation would be equal in knowledge and action. Beginners may imagine the soul’s part like a piece of cloth or a fragment of a body, not realizing that bodily division entails separation into distinct places—like water in a flask or wind in a bellows, where the flask contains the water and the bellows the wind.  

The division of the soul, however, is intelligible. Hence, philosophers discuss the *indivisible part*, as we shall elaborate in later epistles. Whoever studies this book and examines *Majmūʿ al-Ḥikmah* will find that much of what we present is either absent there or insufficiently detailed.  

We assert—based on proof—that the *rational soul* is neither a body nor a bodily faculty. Rather, it is the form of the body, while the body is its instrument. All knowledge is either *perceived* (sensory) or *intelligible*. Sensory knowledge, like most crafts, relates to the body and resides in the imagination and memory. Intelligible knowledge, however, does not enter the imagination but resides in the rational soul. There is no doubt that the intelligible and the intellect are one. Thus, this intelligible was not originally in the soul but was acquired through deduction or instruction, becoming one with it.  

Therefore, the soul’s knowledge and its indivisible part both exist—but in the intellect, not the senses. Plato’s soul-form in the universal soul is like Plato’s knowledge-form in his individual soul. This differentiation and division are intellectual, not sensory—and this is the proof.

### **Chapter: On Motion**  

We say motion is of six kinds:  
1. Generation and corruption (*kawn wa fasād*)  
2. Increase and decrease (*ziyādah wa nuqṣān*)  
3. Alteration and displacement (*taghayyur wa fiʿl*)  

**Generation** occurs in two ways: from non-existence to existence, or from potentiality to actuality. **Corruption** is the opposite—for example, a date-palm grows from a date-seed, and when it burns, it undergoes corruption. The tree’s substance arises from water and earth; water nourishes the soil, which forms the seed’s paste, shaped by nature into a tree. When the tree decays, water returns to water, earth to earth, and the natural form dissolves into its elements.  

What the Necessary Being creates—such as the soul, intellect, and celestial spheres—never perishes, for their forms are immutable. In contrast, earthly forms (like those of plants and animals) are unstable; their matter and form separate over time.  

**Example from the lower world:** Gold and lead. Gold’s moisture and oiliness are so firmly fixed in its earthly parts that neither fire nor time can destroy it. Lead, however, is consumed by fire in an hour, its liquidity and oiliness returning to their origins, while its earthy parts revert to soil (what people call *mardāsang*). The same applies to ruby and alum.  

If it is possible for a substance in the lower world to resist separation of form from matter across ages, how much more so for that which is beyond generation and corruption? For example, burning a tree (or any plant, animal, or mineral) does not destroy fire itself—rather, the fire returns to its origin.  

**Increase** is the growth of a body due to external addition, like water expanding in a flask near fire, or wind filling a bellows until it bursts.  

**Decrease** is the reduction of a thing as its parts diminish.  

**Alteration** is the change of an attribute in a subject, such as color, taste, or smell.  

**Displacement** is forced motion, whereas rotational motion is distinct.  

Motion varies: sometimes the interval between motion and rest is apparent, other times hidden (as in music, where a swift note may seem continuous). Some motions arise from a body’s nature (like fire’s upward movement or an animal’s life-force), implanted by the soul. Other motions require an external mover, whether forced or circular, and occur in six directions: up, down, right, left, front, and back.

### **Chapter: On Time**  

Time is the state between the past and future, measured by the celestial sphere’s rotation. Many define time thus, though some definitions are invalid. Time exists on earth at every point where the celestial sphere’s cycle is present—for nowhere on earth is devoid of this cycle.  

Know that time belongs to the earth, not the heavens. Though the earth’s shadow reaches Mercury’s sphere, darkness does not pervade the entire celestial rotation. Where there is no darkness, there is no time. Thus, time is earthly.
