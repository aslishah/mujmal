### | A Summary of All the Epistles on Theology in a Single Treatise  
In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.  
Know that here we have presented a treatise on various schools of thought, as well as on belief, disposition, and mutual support. In this treatise, there is no bias, partisanship, or discord. Therefore, we deemed it proper to compile one chapter divided into ten sections, in each of which we shall present what is fitting for the sections of those ten epistles—concerning the beliefs, dispositions, and methods of the philosophers, and that to which they devote themselves from the beginning to the end of their lives. What we have presented in this treatise is entirely free of obscurity, and whatever is in this book—we have included in accordance with the beliefs of the people of wisdom. If anything inclined toward another perspective, we paid it no heed, nor did we bring forth arguments, for tales and fables are abundant. Truth must prevail.  

### || First Section  
Know that in these epistles, we have recounted many of the beliefs of the philosophers, though not in an ordered manner.  
-319-  
Here, we shall summarize them concisely. Belief is a path upon which people must stand and by which they may seek their ultimate return. This is the true meaning of belief, just as opinion, doctrine, religion, and sacred law are likewise. Except that sacred law is the disposition of a sent prophet or messenger, and through religion and sacred law, all that we have mentioned is sought. The foundation of this belief must be sound contemplation upon the nature of the world—that which is called "existence," from the lowest to the most exalted, from the circumference of the greatest sphere to the center of the earth. This contemplation cannot be undertaken unless one first casts aside all preconceived beliefs and becomes free from the totality of opinions. If the seeker of knowledge is capable, it is well; if not, he must place himself in the hands of a trusted physician-teacher, submitting himself so that the teacher may examine all his principal and non-principal faculties, determining which of the four humors dominates—especially black bile and burnt blood, which the learned physicians call "melancholia"—until, with the aid of a skilled physician, the excess of that ailment is removed and balance is restored. Then, he must withdraw from society for a month or less, devoting himself to bodily discipline, ensuring that no trace of anger or desire remains within him, making peace with his inner self, and casting aside all religious and philosophical beliefs. Only when he has attained this state will his contemplation be sound. If he wishes to perceive truth, truth will reveal itself to him, for contemplation cannot be achieved except by this method—and this is the only way, without doubt.  

If, in his time, he finds a teacher who embodies the qualities we have described, he must attach himself to that teacher and learn from him the path of his origin and return. Such a man must be fair in appearance, virtuous in character, humble, and knowledgeable in all sciences—both those pertaining to sacred law and those pertaining to philosophy. He must be free from hostility, avoid harming others, and live in seclusion. If someone wrongs him, he must respond with kindness rather than retaliation. He must not crave worldly accumulation,  
-320-  
must shun lust, and must not entangle himself in the burdens of family or worldly attachments. He must never accept anything from God’s creatures on account of his knowledge, and if compelled, he should seek only enough for sustenance. When a man is thus perfected, he will have no need for the world, will not strike others, will not teach the unworthy, nor withhold knowledge from those who deserve it, and will never speak or listen to evil. If these qualities are found in a man, he may be taken as a teacher.  

If a disciple has undergone spiritual discipline and finds such a teacher, he may place his trust in him, for with such a teacher, further discipline is unnecessary. But if no teacher is found, discipline is essential, for whichever humor dominates a person will incline him toward corresponding beliefs—and this cannot be discussed here. The degree of discipline we have prescribed is exceedingly brief, for it is well-known through continuous tradition how the Greeks attained knowledge. The least of their discipline was gradually reducing their food intake, diminishing their primary sustenance by three dirhams daily until they reached thirty dirhams. Then, for a month, they maintained that amount, and from thirty dirhams, they reduced one daily until they reached ten. Their food consisted of boiled chickpeas with almond oil. For forty days, they subsisted on ten dirhams daily, and during those forty days, they deduced whatever knowledge they sought—such as Euclid’s geometry, arithmetic, music, talismans, the properties of gems, and other sciences upon which scholars agree they deduced. Compared to their discipline, what we have described is nothing.  

After all this, contemplation must be sound, free from any inclination to the contrary. When one contemplates, he must first reflect upon the instruments of logic, then upon arithmetic, geometry, and astronomy, seeking to understand the nature of the higher and lower worlds and to grasp their reality. Then he must proceed to the philosophical sciences, discerning the causes of things, investigating origins and ends, and refraining from adhering to any belief until truth reveals itself. The divine philosophers have affirmed that such a belief is assuredly the means of salvation.  

Thus, we say: Know that contemplation is either of oneself or of that which is other than oneself. When a person contemplates himself, he sees a body and a soul attached to it. We have given many proofs that the body is of the earthly world and the soul of the world of spirits. The body he knows was not, then came to be, and the soul—if fairness is granted—was likewise not, then came to be. For if it had existed before, now that it knows itself, it would know where and how it was before. Since, despite many proofs, it cannot discern its own origin, it follows that the existence of the soul was bound to the body. We have said that man is a microcosm, and the universe is the macrocosm of man. Thus, in the existence of the universe lies the existence of his body. Both are originated, and no originated being exists without an originator. The originator of the universe is its Maker. Whoever says the universe has a Maker must ask: Where was the creation? And if the creation was, where was the Maker? For creation without a creator is impossible, just as writing without a writer is impossible. Whoever claims that a universe of such grandeur, with all its wonders, could exist without a Maker must also accept that a book or knowledge could exist without a writer—and this is pure absurdity.  

From this, know that as we have said in previous epistles: Nothing existed except the Necessary Being, and the Necessary Being was necessary through itself, not through another. All other things that exist are, in themselves, contingent—for their cause is the Necessary Being, and the cause of the Necessary Being is itself. For it may be said that two comes from one, but it cannot be said that one comes from something, or that another existed before one. All numbers, extending infinitely, arise from one, and one itself is eternal—one precedes all, and nothing precedes one. By this proof, know that all existents other than the Necessary Being did not exist, then came to be, while the Necessary Being has always existed without beginning.  

Since number is infinite and all numbers arise from one, one itself has no end. Likewise, all existents arise from the Necessary Being. Thus, existence has no end, and the Necessary Being is not attached to anything. Just as one sustains all numbers, the Necessary Being sustains all existents. If this proposition seems difficult, it may be deduced from the premises we have mentioned, with no need for further arbitration.  

As for the differences among the schools of the philosophers, know that their disagreements are not in the principles. If they differ in secondary matters, it is not a disagreement that leads to corruption. For them, the principle is knowing the Necessary Being, and the secondary matters concern the nature of the universe.  

Plato, in recognizing the Necessary Being, articulated doctrines of unity that remain unparalleled among the learned. In the sciences pertaining to principles and secondary matters, he revealed causes that none before him had disclosed. In secondary matters, he speaks in a way that does not admit of annihilation, presenting two demonstrative syllogisms concerning generation and corruption. He says the Necessary Being is not compelled to act, for It has no need, and whatever It does is out of pure mercy—absolute generosity. That Its act is not necessitated is because necessity arises in three ways:  
-321-  
1. By nature, as fire burns, air moves, and water flows. The act of the Necessary Being cannot be of this kind, for we agree that the heavens act by specific properties, not by nature—since they have no nature. How then could the act of the Necessary Being be of this kind?  
2. By compulsion, when another forces one to act—as a master does a servant, a king his subjects, a father his child, or a teacher his student. The Necessary Being is the cause of all existents, and nothing precedes It to compel It to act.  
3. By need, which has many aspects. Need may arise from seeking benefit (as a teacher needs a student) or from seeking sustenance (as a laborer does). There are many other aspects, but when we speak of need, it is clear that the Necessary Being has no need for anyone, and Its act cannot arise from need. If other aspects are proposed, they all fall under these three.  

Thus, the act of the Necessary Being is not of these kinds. Other possibilities divide into two: either hope for recompense (such as repayment, wages, gratitude, joy, or praise—when one does good and expects no return, joy arises in the soul of the benefactor). As long as joy is seen, it is as if recompense has been received. But this cannot be said of the Necessary Being and is invalid. Therefore, Its generosity is absolute mercy, which the philosophers call pure beneficence.  

By this sound syllogism, it is established that the act of the Necessary Being is not by nature, recompense, need, or compulsion—but by mercy. This is a conditional disjunctive syllogism.  

Thus, we say the nature of the universe arises in no more than two ways: either it coexists eternally with the Necessary Being, or it does not, and after a determined time, the act occurs and it comes into being. If we say it coexists eternally, then the universe would itself be the Necessary Being—and this is impossible. If we say it comes into being after a determined time, such an act must be by will—and will is a state that arises in someone after not having been. Such a state is unbecoming of the Necessary Being. We shall elaborate on this in recounting the beliefs of the philosophers.
