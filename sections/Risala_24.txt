### | خلاصۀ رسالۀ نهم از طبيعيّات در حاسّ‌ و محسوس
بسم اللّه الرحمن الرحيم 
بدان‌كه علم مردم به موجودات از سه طريق باشد:- اول از طريق «حواسّ‌ پنج‌گانه» كه آن اول طريق است به معلومات، و مردم با اطفال و بعضى از حيوانات درين طريق شريك‌اند.- دوم طريق «عقل» است به مقدمات اوليّات، و مردم درين طريق با اطفال و حيوان غيرناطق شريك نيستند. ولكن عالم و غير عالم در اين شريك باشند.- سيوم طريق «برهان» است و اين خاص حكما را باشد و معرفت بدين طريق بعد از معرفت علوم رياضت و هندسه و منطق باشد و هريك به‌جاى خويش گفتيم. و «محسوسات» را به حسّ‌ در توان يافت و معقولات اولى را به عقل مجرّد و برهان به آلتى كه عقل بيند و آن منطق است. اما حواس آلتهايى باشند جسدانى و آن چشم و گوش و بينى و زبان و دست است به مشاركت همه تن. اما قوّت حسّاسه به قوّت روحانى باشد و شايد كه «نفسانى» گويند و اين قوّتها در هر عضوى فعلى كند.

### Summary of the Ninth Epistle on Natural Sciences Concerning the Senses and the Sensed  
In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful  

Know that human knowledge of existent beings is attained through three means:  
1. The first is through the *five senses*, which is the primary means of acquiring knowledge. In this, humans share with children and some animals.  
2. The second is through *intellect*, by means of primary premises. Here, humans do not share with children or non-rational animals, though the learned and the ignorant do share in this.  
3. The third is through *demonstration*, which is exclusive to the philosophers. Knowledge through this means comes after mastering the sciences of mathematics, geometry, and logic, each of which we have discussed in its proper place.  

The *sensed* can be perceived through the senses, primary intelligibles through the abstract intellect, and demonstration through the instrument that the intellect employs—namely, logic.  

As for the senses, their instruments are corporeal: the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and hand, in collaboration with the entire body. However, the faculty of sensation is a spiritual power—some may call it *psychic*—and these faculties act within each organ.  

-197-  

The sensed are accidents subsisting in body and composite substance—some may refer to it simply as *body*, others as *composite substance*. These accidents affect the five senses in terms of quantity and quality.  

The faculty of vision resides in seven layers and three humors, as we have mentioned. The faculty of hearing lies within the ear, in a membrane stretched there. The faculty of smell is in a hollow nerve within the nose, resembling two nipple-like protrusions, its cavity like a honeycomb. Sometimes, when old ox or mule bones are broken, cavities appear within them resembling the cavities of the olfactory organ. The faculty of taste lies in a moisture spread over the tongue, and the faculty of touch is distributed throughout the body, though it is strongest in the hands.  

The sensed objects are of five kinds:  
1. The first is perceived by *touch*, which has ten types: heat, cold, dryness, moisture, roughness, smoothness, hardness, softness, lightness, and heaviness.  
2. The second is perceived by *taste*, which is flavor, of nine kinds: sweetness, sourness, bitterness, saltiness, fattiness, pungency, pleasantness, astringency, and acidity.  
3. The third is perceived by *smell*, of two kinds: pleasant and unpleasant.  
4. The fourth is *hearing*, also of two kinds: animal and non-animal. The non-animal is further divided into natural (e.g., the sound of stone, iron, wood, thunder, or wind) and artificial (e.g., the sound of drums, horns, flutes, or silk). The animal is divided into rational (human speech) and non-rational (sounds of non-speaking animals). Rational speech is itself of two kinds: demonstrative and non-demonstrative.  
5. The fifth is *vision*, which perceives ten things: light, darkness, color, body, surface, shape, position, distance, motion, and rest.  

-198-  

The faculty of touch perceives heat and cold such that when a warm object is encountered, the natural heat in touch increases and overcomes the coldness within, allowing the soul to perceive it. Conversely, when a cold object is encountered, the natural coldness of the animal increases until it overcomes the innate heat of the human, so the imaginative faculty in the brain becomes aware. If the object’s heat or cold does not oppose the human body’s temperament, no sensory effect occurs. However, it is not devoid of two aspects: either the object is rougher or smoother than the human body, and thus the sense perceives it. If it matches the body in smoothness or roughness—whether of the same kind (like a child or a servant) or not (like a friend, brother, or kin)—then if it is a locus of desire, the organs of desire are aroused; otherwise, the sense does not perceive it. Yet rarely does an object encountered by touch match the human body in heat, cold, smoothness, or roughness—except the body itself.  

Similarly, if an object is hard, the human recoils from its harshness. Some hard objects absorb, like wood in water, clay, or dough, and the tactile faculty, through the psychic power of the brain, perceives its hardness. The same applies to the other faculties.  

As for dryness and wetness, when a dry object meets the human body, the moisture in the pores is scattered by the dominance of the dry object, and the sense perceives it. If it is wet, the moisture in the pores increases upon contact, becoming perceptible.  

Heaviness and lightness are perceived by the power in the nerves and muscles.  

The faculty of taste operates when something contacts the moisture of the tongue, mixing with it according to its temperament. This moisture seeps into the tongue’s pores, conveying the nine flavors we detailed. Beneath the tongue’s skin is a muscular layer connected to the brain.  

-199-  

The faculty of smell perceives when a fine vapor dissolves from bodies and mingles with air, forming a subtle, spiritual temperament. The air becomes like vapor—pleasant if the scent is pleasant, unpleasant if otherwise. Any animal with lungs inhales this air through the olfactory organ (the nipple-like protrusions mentioned earlier), and the air in the cavity takes on the scent’s quality, which the sense then perceives. If the air is pleasant, the sensory soul delights in it; if unpleasant, aversion arises.  

Note that animals differ in their perception of pleasant and unpleasant smells. For instance, a beetle dies from the scent of flowers but thrives on dung, while a dung-beetle is distressed by perfume but pleased by filth. These differences stem from temperaments and dominant bodily humors.  

The faculty of hearing perceives two kinds: animal and non-animal. Non-animal sounds are either natural (like stone, iron, wood, thunder, or wind) or artificial (like drums, horns, or flutes). Animal sounds are either rational (human speech) or non-rational (non-speaking animals). Rational speech is further divided: that which signifies something (meaningful speech) and that which does not (like laughter or crying, which lack syllabic structure).  

All sound arises from the striking or friction of two bodies, creating waves in the air that travel according to the force of impact. If the auditory faculty intercepts these waves, the composite power in the ear perceives them and informs the imagination.  

The analogy of air waves is like a stone dropped in water: circles spread outward, gradually dissipating. However, air waves expand in six directions, unlike water’s surface waves. The sound weakens over distance, just as a bow’s tension lessens the more it is drawn.  

-200-  

Hearing functions day and night, unaffected by light or darkness, unlike vision, which fails in darkness. Hearing can perceive through barriers, whereas vision cannot. Thus, many philosophers rank hearing above sight. The Quran also mentions hearing first, indicating its greater nobility due to its wider utility.  

The human eye perceives darkness but sees nothing in it; in daylight, it perceives light and, through light, other objects. Light is perceived directly, as whiteness resembles light and blackness resembles darkness. No color reveals itself as purely as black or white.  

Light and darkness in the world’s bodies are like the soul in the body. If bodies are pure and unclouded, the soul acts in them as light does in transparent objects like crystal or glass—a clear analogy.  

When light strikes a body’s surface, if transparent, it reflects; if not, the light’s reflection takes on the body’s color, as in mirrors. Different colors are perceived simultaneously, just as touch discerns rough and smooth, hot and cold, hard and soft without contradiction.  

Know that these faculties in humans are like hands, feet, and head—each a distinct organ with separate functions, yet all governed by a single soul. When it acts in hearing, it is called *auditory*; in sight, *visual*; in the liver, *natural*; in the brain, *vital soul*. Just as the First Creation by God is called *substance*, when it governs bodies, it is called *soul*; when it knows the meanings of existents, it is *intellect*; when it governs plants, *vegetative soul*; when it governs humans, *human soul*; when it manages the liver, *natural soul*. Thus, it is named according to its function.  

The universal soul of the world is one, its light a single light. When it acts in plants, it transforms them; in animals, it grants life. The action is one, but its name changes with its locus—whether in the sensed or the intelligible.
