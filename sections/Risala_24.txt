### Summary of the Eleventh Epistle on Natural Philosophy Concerning the Composition of the Body  
*In the Name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful*  

Know that this epistle has discussed this matter elsewhere and repeats it here, though we did not intend to reiterate it. We have included in this epistle certain points—whether astronomical or philosophical—that pertain to it.  

When wise men observed, through both sensory perception and rational inquiry, they found nothing in the lower world (the sublunary realm) comparable to the composition of the bodily form and its essential qualities as found in man. Likewise, when they examined the higher world (the celestial realm), which is the domain of order and harmony, every effect they witnessed there had its counterpart in man.  

First, they observed beings akin to themselves—namely, the animal kind. They saw that some animals had no occupation other than eating, sleeping, copulating, and indulging in pleasure and vigilance. They recognized these same traits within themselves and their own kind. Similarly, they noted procreation, mating, childbirth, nurturing offspring, protecting their young, affection for mates, and all that pertains to mates and children—all of which they found within themselves.  

Then they considered another kind—the vegetal realm—and found that it possessed seeds which, when exposed to heat, were "cooked," nourished by water, and thus grew. Looking within themselves, they saw that they, too, could not do without nourishment—food and water. From infancy until around thirty-five years of age, they observed their own growth, after which they declined until the time of death and annihilation. They likewise saw that animals and plants perished, their faculties dissolving, each returning to its origin. They found their own state to be the same: just as plants and animals, after the dissolution of their constituent elements, merged with the earth, so too did they—whether virtuous or wicked, ignorant or philosopher—meet the same fate without distinction.  

When they had observed these meanings across different kinds, they examined distinct species. For example, in the category of wild beasts, they saw divisions based on form and characteristics—such as the lion, wolf, leopard, bear, pig, dog, and fox, and the like. In the lion, they saw pride; in the wolf, malice; in the leopard, lust; in the pig, docility; in the bear, gluttony and desire; in the dog, hostility; in the fox, cunning. All these traits they found in the human species, with a single individual sometimes embodying them all, as discerned through keen insight.  

Similarly, in the category of creeping creatures—such as the snake and scorpion—they observed malice in the snake and arrogance in the scorpion, all of which they also saw in mankind.  

Then they turned their gaze to the earth and beheld a sphere upon which water flowed, both upon its surface and within it, giving rise to vegetation. They saw themselves as akin to the earth, with blood coursing through their veins like water through channels, and hair upon their limbs like plants. Likewise, all that they observed in minerals—such as salt, alum, and other substances found in caves and mountains—they also found in man: the saltiness of sweat, the bitterness of urine, the marrow within bones, and the like.  

When they examined regions, cities, and mines, they found them all analogous to man. The region was like the body; the cities, like its limbs; the shops, like the senses; and the craftsmen, like the faculties within it.  

Since they found all that existed in the earth reflected in man—as stated in other epistles—they called the universe the *Macrocosm* ("Great Man") and man the *Microcosm* ("Small Universe"), for everything in the universe was manifest in man, each aspect corresponding to a faculty derived from it.  

Thus, they contemplated the totality of body and soul, observing what actions arose from them. They found the soul to be the animating force that gave life to the entire body. The effects of this soul, which governed man, they also perceived in the celestial sphere, the universal soul, and the intellect—each action and state corresponding to another. Hence, they declared:  

**"Man is a microcosm, and the universe is a macrocosm."**  

*And God knows best.*

### Notes on Translation:  
1. **Philosophical Nuances**: The text draws heavily on Neoplatonic and Aristotelian concepts, particularly the correspondence between macrocosm (universe) and microcosm (man).  
2. **Terminology**:  
   - *ʿĀlam al-suflā* (عالم سفلى) = "sublunary world" (the earthly, material realm).  
   - *ʿĀlam al-ʿulwā* (عالم علوى) = "celestial world" (the higher, spiritual realm).  
   - *Insān kabīr* (انسان كبير) = "Macrocosm" (the universe as a "Great Man").  
   - *ʿĀlam ṣaghīr* (عالم صغير) = "Microcosm" (man as a "Small Universe").  
3. **Stylistic Choices**: The translation preserves the repetitive, contemplative style of the original, which mirrors the Brethren's methodical approach to knowledge.
